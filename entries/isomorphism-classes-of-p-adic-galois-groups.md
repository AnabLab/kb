**Statement.** Let $K, L$ be finite extensions of $\mathbb{Q}_p$ in a fixed algebraic closure, and let $K^0, L^0$ be their maximal subfields abelian over $\mathbb{Q}_p$.

1. For every $p$, if $G_K \cong G_L$, then $[K:\mathbb{Q}_p] = [L:\mathbb{Q}_p]$ [@jardenritter1979, Lemma 1] and $K^0 = L^0$ [@jenkner1992, Théorème].
2. Suppose that $p$ is odd, or that $p = 2$ and $K(\sqrt{-1})/K$ is unramified, which includes the case $\sqrt{-1} \in K$. If $[K:\mathbb{Q}_p] = [L:\mathbb{Q}_p]$ and $K^0 = L^0$, then $G_K \cong G_L$.

For $p = 2$, whether $K(\sqrt{-1})/K$ is unramified depends only on $K^0$, by Step 1 below. So in the cases of part 2, the isomorphism type of $G_K$ carries exactly the information of the pair $([K:\mathbb{Q}_p], K^0)$. For $p = 2$ with $K(\sqrt{-1})/K$ ramified, part 2 is settled here only in special cases. See the last two sections.

**Attribution.** Jarden and Ritter proved both parts when $\zeta_p \in K$, and $\zeta_4 \in K$ if $p = 2$ (the [Jarden–Ritter theorem](jarden-ritter-theorem.md)). Ritter's paper continuing that work proves both parts for every odd $p$ [@ritter1978, Theorem, p. 281], deducing part 2 from a result of Koch [@ritter1978, pp. 286–288]. Joshi states the same equivalence and credits Ritter [@joshi2020, §3.5 and Theorem 3.5.1]. Jenkner proves part 1 for every $p$, using only local class field theory [@jenkner1992, pp. 221–225]. He also remarks that Jannsen–Wingberg and Diekert describe $G_K$ when $K(\zeta_{2p})/K$ has no wild ramification, and that the results above, including Ritter's, follow from these descriptions fairly directly [@jenkner1992, p. 221]. For $p = 2$ that hypothesis says that $K(\sqrt{-1})/K$ is unramified, since a ramified quadratic extension in residue characteristic $2$ is wildly ramified. The proof of part 2 below is written out here from those presentations.

**Proof of part 2.** For a finite extension $K/\mathbb{Q}_p$, write $f_K$ for its residue degree and $N_K = N_{K/\mathbb{Q}_p}(K^\times) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}_p^\times$ for its norm group. Let $T_K$ be its maximal tamely ramified extension, $\mathcal{G}_K = \mathrm{Gal}(T_K/K)$ and $I_K^t = \mathrm{Gal}(T_K/K^{\mathrm{ur}})$. Let $p^{s_K}$ be the order of the group of $p$-power roots of unity in $T_K$, and $\alpha_K : \mathcal{G}_K \to (\mathbb{Z}/p^{s_K})^\times$ the character by which $\mathcal{G}_K$ acts on it. Put $H_K = \alpha_K(I_K^t)$, and let $\Phi_K$ be the set of values $\alpha_K(\sigma)$ for $\sigma \in \mathcal{G}_K$ a lift of Frobenius. Write $\phi$ for local Artin maps, $\chi$ for the $p$-adic cyclotomic character, and $c = \chi \circ \phi_{\mathbb{Q}_p} : \mathbb{Q}_p^\times \to \mathbb{Z}_p^\times$.

*Step 1: $f_K$, $s_K$, $H_K$ and $\Phi_K$ depend only on $K^0$.* This step works for every $p$. Since $v_p(N_{K/\mathbb{Q}_p}(x)) = f_K v_K(x)$, we get $v_p(N_K) = f_K \mathbb{Z}$, $N_{K/\mathbb{Q}_p}(\mathcal{O}_K^\times) = N_K \cap \mathbb{Z}_p^\times$, and $N_{K/\mathbb{Q}_p}(\pi_K \mathcal{O}_K^\times) = \{y \in N_K : v_p(y) = f_K\}$ for a uniformizer $\pi_K$. In $\mathrm{Gal}(K^{\mathrm{ab}}/K)$, the image of inertia is $\phi_K(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)$, and the elements acting as Frobenius on $K^{\mathrm{ur}}$ form $\phi_K(\pi_K \mathcal{O}_K^\times)$ [@milnecft, I, Theorem 1.1 and 1.10–1.11]. Moreover $\chi(\phi_K(x)) = c(N_{K/\mathbb{Q}_p}(x))$ for $x \in K^\times$ [@milnecft, III, Lemma 3.2]. Hence:

- $f_K$ is the positive generator of $v_p(N_K)$.
- $\mu_{p^\nu} \subset T_K$ exactly when the inertia group of $K(\mu_{p^\nu})/K$ has order prime to $p$. The character $\chi$ maps that inertia group isomorphically onto the image of $c(N_K \cap \mathbb{Z}_p^\times)$ in $(\mathbb{Z}/p^\nu)^\times$. So $s_K$ is the largest $\nu$ for which this image has order prime to $p$.
- $H_K$ is the image of $c(N_K \cap \mathbb{Z}_p^\times)$ in $(\mathbb{Z}/p^{s_K})^\times$, and $\Phi_K$ is the image of $c(\{y \in N_K : v_p(y) = f_K\})$, a coset of $H_K$.

So these data depend only on $N_K$, and $N_K = N_{K^0}$ by the norm limitation theorem [@milnecft, III, Theorem 3.5].

For $p = 2$, the group $H_K$ has odd order and lies in the $2$-group $(\mathbb{Z}/2^{s_K})^\times$, so it is trivial and $\Phi_K$ has a single element. Also, $\mu_4 \subset T_K$ exactly when $K(\sqrt{-1})/K$ is tamely ramified, which for an extension of degree at most $2$ means unramified. So $s_K \ge 2$ exactly when $K(\sqrt{-1})/K$ is unramified, and this depends only on $K^0$.

*Step 2: choice of generators.* The group $\mathcal{G}_K$ has a presentation with generators $\sigma_0, \tau_0$ and the single relation $\sigma_0 \tau_0 \sigma_0^{-1} = \tau_0^q$, where $q = p^{f_K}$, $\tau_0$ generates $I_K^t$ and $\sigma_0$ lifts Frobenius [@luo2024, Proposition 2.15], which cites Neukirch–Schmidt–Wingberg, Theorem 7.5.3. Any other lift of Frobenius is $\sigma_0 \tau_0^a$, and any other generator of $I_K^t$ is $\tau_0^u$, with $a \in \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ and $u \in \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}^\times$. The substitution $\sigma \mapsto \sigma\tau^a$, $\tau \mapsto \tau^u$ preserves the relation and has inverse $\sigma \mapsto \sigma\tau^{-a/u}$, $\tau \mapsto \tau^{1/u}$. So any lift of Frobenius together with any generator of $I_K^t$ gives such a presentation.

*Step 3: matching.* Suppose $[K:\mathbb{Q}_p] = [L:\mathbb{Q}_p] = n$ and $K^0 = L^0$. By Step 1, $f_K = f_L$, $s_K = s_L = s$, $H_K = H_L = H$ and $\Phi_K = \Phi_L$. Choose a generator $\tau_K$ of $I_K^t$. The images $\alpha_K(\tau_K)$ and $\alpha_L(\tau_L)$ of any generators both generate the cyclic group $H$, so after replacing $\tau_L$ by a power $\tau_L^u$ with $u \in \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}^\times$, we have $\alpha_L(\tau_L) = \alpha_K(\tau_K)$. Choose a lift $\sigma_K$ of Frobenius. Since $\Phi_L = \Phi_K$, there is a lift $\sigma_L$ of Frobenius with $\alpha_L(\sigma_L) = \alpha_K(\sigma_K)$. By Step 2 there is an isomorphism $\psi : \mathcal{G}_K \to \mathcal{G}_L$ with $\psi(\sigma_K) = \sigma_L$ and $\psi(\tau_K) = \tau_L$, and $\alpha_L \circ \psi = \alpha_K$ because both sides agree on generators.

*Step 4: conclusion for odd $p$.* Let $p$ be odd. Jannsen and Wingberg present $G_K$ by the generators $\sigma_K, \tau_K, x_0, \dots, x_n$ with relations built from $n$, $s$, the pair $(\sigma_K, \tau_K)$ and a lift $\beta_K : \mathcal{G}_K \to \mathbb{Z}_p^\times$ of $\alpha_K^{-1}$ [@jannsenwingberg1982, Theorem 2]. Taking $\beta_L = \beta_K \circ \psi^{-1}$, the isomorphism $\psi$ carries the data for $K$ to the data for $L$, so $G_K \cong G_L$.

*Step 5: conclusion for $p = 2$.* Let $p = 2$, suppose that $K(\sqrt{-1})/K$ is unramified, and put $s = s_K$, so that $s \ge 2$ by Step 1. Diekert shows that $G_K$ is isomorphic to the profinite group with generators $\sigma, \tau, x_0, \dots, x_n$, where $n = [K:\mathbb{Q}_2]$, and these defining relations [@diekert1984, Theorem 3.1]: the closed normal subgroup generated by $x_0, \dots, x_n$ is a pro-$2$ group, $\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1} = \tau^{2^{f_K}}$, and $\sigma x_0 \sigma^{-1} = (x_0\tau)^{\pi g} x_1^{2^s} [x_1, x_2] \cdots [x_{n-1}, x_n]$. Here $\pi \in \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ is the element with $\pi\widehat{\mathbb{Z}} = \mathbb{Z}_2$, and $g \in \mathbb{Z}_2$ is such that a Frobenius automorphism acts on $\mu_{2^s}$ by $\zeta \mapsto \zeta^g$. Now let $[L:\mathbb{Q}_2] = n$ and $L^0 = K^0$. By Step 1, $f_L = f_K$ and $s_L = s_K$, so Diekert's theorem applies to $L$ with the same $n$, $f$ and $s$. Since $\Phi_L = \Phi_K$, the Frobenius automorphisms of $L$ also act on $\mu_{2^s}$ by $\zeta \mapsto \zeta^g$, so the same $g$ serves for $L$. The two presentations coincide, and $G_K \cong G_L$.

**The case $p = 2$ with $K(\sqrt{-1})/K$ ramified.** Here Diekert's hypothesis fails. Schneider writes that $G_K$ is still unknown in this case, and that there is no sensible notion of a Demuškin formation for it [@schneider2016, Introduction, p. 1]. Roe and Turturean give a presentation of $G_{\mathbb{Q}_2}$ in a 2026 manuscript, which their project page says has not yet been peer reviewed, and they ask for an extension to every finite $K/\mathbb{Q}_2$ [@roeturturean2026, Theorem 1.2 and §11]. For part 2, though, $K = \mathbb{Q}_2$ is no test, since it is the only extension of degree $1$. Part 2 is also immediate in degrees $2$ and $3$:

- If $K$ is abelian over $\mathbb{Q}_2$, as every quadratic $K$ is, then $K^0 = K$, so $L^0 = K$ and $[L:\mathbb{Q}_2] = [K:\mathbb{Q}_2]$ force $L = K$.
- A cubic $K$ that is not abelian is not unramified, so it is totally ramified. For a uniformizer $\varpi$, then, $\varpi^3 = 2u$ with $u \in 1 + \varpi\mathcal{O}_K$, since the residue field is $\mathbb{F}_2$. This group is pro-$2$, so $u = w^3$ for some $w$ in it, and $(\varpi/w)^3 = 2$. So $K$ is conjugate to $\mathbb{Q}_2(\sqrt[3]{2})$. Then $K^0 = \mathbb{Q}_2$, so $L^0 = \mathbb{Q}_2$ and $L$ is a non-abelian cubic too, hence also conjugate to $\mathbb{Q}_2(\sqrt[3]{2})$. Conjugate fields have isomorphic absolute Galois groups.
- More generally, let $K$ be tamely ramified, that is, of odd ramification index $e$, with residue degree $f$. Then the fields $L$ that part 2 compares with $K$ are the tamely ramified fields with the same $e$ and $f$. If $\gcd(e, 2^f - 1) = 1$, they are all isomorphic to $K$, so part 2 holds for $K$. Every field of odd degree is tamely ramified, and this covers every $K$ of odd degree less than $21$. See [tamely ramified 2-adic fields](tamely-ramified-2-adic-fields.md), which also gives the smallest tame cases left open, in degrees $6$ and $21$.

So the smallest cases left open here have degree $4$. The examples below give one where $K'$, defined next, is real and one where it is not.

*What part 2 asks here.* Let $K' = K \cap \mathbb{Q}_2(\mu_{2^\infty})$, and call it real if $\zeta \mapsto \zeta^{-1}$ fixes it. If $[K:\mathbb{Q}_2]$ is odd, or if $K'$ is not real, then $K^0$ is the compositum of $K'$ and the unramified extension of $\mathbb{Q}_2$ of degree $f_K$, and $f_K$ and $K'$ can in turn be read off from $K^0$ (see [the maximal abelian subfield of a 2-adic field](maximal-abelian-subfields-of-2-adic-fields.md)). For these fields, part 2 says that $G_K$ depends only on $[K:\mathbb{Q}_2]$, $f_K$ and $K'$. Two results point that way.

- The maximal extension of $K$ without simple ramification is the union of the maximal $2$-extensions of the unramified extensions of $K$ of odd degree [@schneider2016, Introduction, pp. 1–2]. Zel'venskii presents its Galois group over $K$ when $[K:\mathbb{Q}_2]$ is odd, and Schneider when the degree is even and $K'$ is not real. Both presentations depend only on the degree and on the largest power $q$ of $2$ with $\mu_q \subset K(\sqrt{-1})$ [@schneider2016, Theorems 2.19 and 2.20]. Roe and Turturean also cite Zel'venskii for the odd case [@roeturturean2026, p. 4]. Whether adjoining $\sqrt{-1}$ ramifies, the case and $q$ all depend only on $K^0$, so for such $K$ this quotient of $G_K$ is the same as for every $L$ with $[L:\mathbb{Q}_2] = [K:\mathbb{Q}_2]$ and $L^0 = K^0$.
- For even degree and $K'$ not real, Schneider conjectures a presentation of $G_K$ itself that depends only on the degree, $f_K$ and $q$. See [Schneider's conjecture](schneider-conjecture-on-2-adic-galois-groups.md), which would prove part 2 for these fields.

When the degree is even and $K'$ is real, $K^0$ carries more information than $f_K$ and $K'$. For example, $\mathbb{Q}_2(\sqrt{10})$ and $\mathbb{Q}_2(\sqrt{-10})$ have the same degree, residue degree, $K' = \mathbb{Q}_2$ and $q = 4$, and adjoining $\sqrt{-1}$ ramifies for both, by the examples in that entry. Yet they are distinct abelian fields, so their absolute Galois groups are not isomorphic, by part 1. So here no description of $G_K$ can depend only on these data.

**Examples.** *Odd $p$.* Let $K = \mathbb{Q}_p(\sqrt[p]{p})$ and $L = \mathbb{Q}_p(\sqrt[p]{p + p^2})$. Both have degree $p$, so neither contains $\zeta_p$, whose degree $p - 1$ does not divide $p$, and the Jarden–Ritter theorem does not apply. Neither is normal over $\mathbb{Q}_p$, since a normal one would contain the ratio $\zeta_p$ of two roots of its defining polynomial $x^p - a$. Having prime degree and not being abelian, both satisfy $K^0 = L^0 = \mathbb{Q}_p$. By part 2, $G_K \cong G_L$.

The fields are not isomorphic, as checked here. A field isomorphism between finite extensions of $\mathbb{Q}_p$ is a $\mathbb{Q}_p$-algebra isomorphism [@hyeon2025, §1], so it would give $K(\zeta_p) = L(\zeta_p)$, because $K(\zeta_p)$ is normal over $\mathbb{Q}_p$. Over $F = \mathbb{Q}_p(\zeta_p)$, Kummer theory and $v_F(p) = p - 1$ would then force $1 + p \in F^{\times p}$. In that case $(1 + p)^{p-1} = N_{F/\mathbb{Q}_p}(1 + p)$ would be a $p$-th power in $\mathbb{Q}_p$. This is impossible: $p$-th powers in $1 + p\mathbb{Z}_p$ lie in $1 + p^2\mathbb{Z}_p$, while $(1 + p)^{p-1} \equiv 1 - p \pmod{p^2}$.

*Quartic $2$-adic fields.* Let $F = \mathbb{Q}_2(\sqrt{c})$ be quadratic, with conjugation $x \mapsto \bar{x}$. By Kummer theory, $a \in \mathbb{Q}_2^\times$ is a square in $F$ exactly when $a$ or $a/c$ is a square in $\mathbb{Q}_2$. Take $s \in F$ with $s\bar{s}$ not a square in $F$. Then $s$ is not a square in $F$, so $F(\sqrt{s})$ has degree $4$ over $\mathbb{Q}_2$. Its conjugates over $\mathbb{Q}_2$ are $F(\sqrt{s})$ and $F(\sqrt{\bar{s}})$, which differ because $s\bar{s}$ is not a square in $F$. So $F(\sqrt{s})$ is not normal over $\mathbb{Q}_2$, and its maximal abelian subfield is $F$. By [@hyeon2025, §1] again, $F(\sqrt{t})$ is isomorphic to $F(\sqrt{s})$ only if $st$ or $\bar{s}t$ is a square in $F$.

- $c = 3$, $K = F(\sqrt{\sqrt{3}}) = \mathbb{Q}_2(\sqrt[4]{3})$ and $L = F(\sqrt{2\sqrt{3}}) = \mathbb{Q}_2(\sqrt[4]{12})$. The norms $-3$ and $-12$ are not squares in $F$, and neither are $\pm 6$, because $-1$, $-3$, $\pm 2$ and $\pm 6$ are not squares in $\mathbb{Q}_2$. So $K$ and $L$ are non-isomorphic, with $K^0 = L^0 = F$. Since $\sqrt{3} \in K$, we have $K(\sqrt{-1}) = K(\sqrt{-3})$, which is unramified over $K$ because $\mathbb{Q}_2(\sqrt{-3}) = \mathbb{Q}_2(\zeta_3)$ is unramified over $\mathbb{Q}_2$, as $x^2 + x + 1$ is irreducible and separable modulo $2$. By part 2, $G_K \cong G_L$. Neither field contains $\sqrt{-1}$, so the Jarden–Ritter theorem does not apply: $\sqrt{-1} \notin F$, and a second quadratic subfield would make the field a compositum of two quadratic fields, hence normal.
- $c = 2$, $K = F(\sqrt{\sqrt{2}}) = \mathbb{Q}_2(\sqrt[4]{2})$ and $L = F(\sqrt{3\sqrt{2}}) = \mathbb{Q}_2(\sqrt[4]{18})$. The norms $-2$ and $-18$ are not squares in $F$, and neither are $\pm 6$, because $-1$, $\pm 3$, $\pm 2$ and $\pm 6$ are not squares in $\mathbb{Q}_2$. So $K$ and $L$ are non-isomorphic, with $K^0 = L^0 = F$. Here $F(\sqrt{-1}) = \mathbb{Q}_2(\zeta_8)$ is totally ramified of degree $4$ over $\mathbb{Q}_2$, since $\zeta_8 - 1$ is a root of the Eisenstein polynomial $(x + 1)^4 + 1$. So $F(\sqrt{-1})/F$ is ramified, and by Step 1, applied to $K$ and $F$, so is $K(\sqrt{-1})/K$. Here $K' = F$ is real, since $\zeta \mapsto \zeta^{-1}$ fixes $\sqrt{2} = \zeta_8 + \zeta_8^{-1}$, so neither result in the previous section applies. Whether $G_K \cong G_L$ is not settled here.
- $c = -2$, $K = F(\sqrt{\sqrt{-2}}) = \mathbb{Q}_2(\sqrt[4]{-2})$ and $L = F(\sqrt{3\sqrt{-2}}) = \mathbb{Q}_2(\sqrt[4]{-18})$. The norms $2$ and $18$ are not squares in $F$, and neither are $\pm 6$, because $2$, $-1$, $\pm 3$ and $\pm 6$ are not squares in $\mathbb{Q}_2$. So $K$ and $L$ are non-isomorphic, with $K^0 = L^0 = F$. As in the previous example, $F(\sqrt{-1}) = \mathbb{Q}_2(\zeta_8)$, so $K(\sqrt{-1})/K$ is ramified. Also $q = 8$, because $K(\sqrt{-1}) \cap \mathbb{Q}_2^{\mathrm{ab}} = F(\sqrt{-1})$ by part 3 of [the maximal abelian subfield of a 2-adic field](maximal-abelian-subfields-of-2-adic-fields.md). Here $K' = F$ is not real, since $\zeta \mapsto \zeta^{-1}$ maps $\sqrt{-2} = \zeta_8 + \zeta_8^3$ to $-\sqrt{-2}$. So the Galois groups of the maximal extensions of $K$ and $L$ without simple ramification are isomorphic, by Schneider's Theorem 2.20 above, and Schneider's conjecture predicts $G_K \cong G_L$. Whether $G_K \cong G_L$ is not settled here.
