**Setting.** Let $K$ be a number field and $Y/K$ a smooth projective geometrically connected curve of genus $g \ge 2$, with fundamental exact sequence $1 \to \pi_1(Y_{\bar K}) \to \pi_1(Y) \to G_K \to 1$. Write $\mathrm{Sec}(Y/K)$ for its set of sections up to conjugation by $\pi_1(Y_{\bar K})$. For a place $u$ of $K$, a section $s$ restricts to a section $s|_{G_u}$ of the local sequence over the decomposition group $G_u$. Betts and Stix call $s$ *Selmer* if, for every place $u$, $s|_{G_u}$ is the section of some point $y_u \in Y(K_u)$. They write $\mathrm{Sec}^{\mathrm{Sel}}(Y/K)$ for the set of Selmer sections, and, for a finite place $u$, $\mathrm{loc}_u : \mathrm{Sec}^{\mathrm{Sel}}(Y/K) \to Y(K_u)$ for the map sending $s$ to the unique such point [@bettsstix2025, Definition 1.1, p. 1]. The point is unique because distinct $K_u$-points give non-conjugate sections when $K_u$ is finite over $\mathbb{Q}_p$; see [injectivity in the section conjecture](section-conjecture-injectivity.md).

By functoriality, the section of a rational point $y \in Y(K)$ restricts on $G_u$ to the section of $y$ regarded as a $K_u$-point. So it is Selmer, and $\mathrm{loc}_u$ sends it to $y$.

**Theorem A (Betts–Stix).** Suppose that $K$ contains no CM subfield. Then for every finite place $v$ of $K$, the image of $\mathrm{loc}_v : \mathrm{Sec}^{\mathrm{Sel}}(Y/K) \to Y(K_v)$ is finite [@bettsstix2025, Theorem A, p. 1].

Totally real fields and fields of odd degree contain no CM subfield [@bettsstix2025, Remark 2.18 (1), p. 13].

- *A prediction of the section conjecture.* If the [section conjecture](section-conjecture.md) holds for $Y$, every section comes from a rational point. Then the image of $\mathrm{loc}_v$ is $Y(K)$, which is finite by Faltings' theorem. Theorem A verifies this consequence unconditionally [@bettsstix2025, Abstract, p. 1].
- *Images only.* Theorem A bounds the image of each $\mathrm{loc}_v$, not $\mathrm{Sec}^{\mathrm{Sel}}(Y/K)$ itself. The section and Mordell conjectures together predict that $\mathrm{Sec}(Y/K)$ is finite. The authors note that this is known only in a handful of examples where the sequence has no sections at all [@bettsstix2025, §1, p. 1].
- *Mordell.* The image of $\mathrm{loc}_v$ contains $Y(K)$, so Theorem A contains Faltings' theorem for such $K$. The authors observe that the finiteness half of the proof, Theorem 6.5 below, already implies the Mordell conjecture, as in Lawrence and Venkatesh's proof [@bettsstix2025, p. 44].

**Theorem B: the finite descent locus.** The modified adelic points $Y(\mathbb{A}_K)_\bullet$ are the product of the finite adelic points $\prod_{v \nmid \infty} Y(K_v)$ with $\prod_{v \mid \infty} \pi_0(Y(K_v))$. Stoll's finite descent locus $Y(\mathbb{A}_K)^{\mathrm{f\text{-}cov}}_\bullet \subseteq Y(\mathbb{A}_K)_\bullet$ contains $Y(K)$, and Stoll conjectures that it equals $Y(K)$. Betts and Stix take from Harari and Stix that it is exactly the image of $\mathrm{Sec}^{\mathrm{Sel}}(Y/K)$ under the total localization map [@bettsstix2025, §1, p. 2]. The interpolation theorem behind this is recorded in [descent and Brauer–Manin obstructions for Galois sections](descent-obstructions-for-galois-sections.md). So Theorem A gives the following: if $K$ contains no CM subfield, then for every finite place $v$ the projection of $Y(\mathbb{A}_K)^{\mathrm{f\text{-}cov}}_\bullet$ to $Y(K_v)$ is finite [@bettsstix2025, Theorem B, p. 2].

**The Lawrence–Venkatesh locus.** An *abelian-by-finite family* $X \to Y' \to Y$ is a surjective finite étale covering $Y' \to Y$ together with a polarized abelian scheme $X \to Y'$ [@bettsstix2025, Definition 4.1, p. 32]. For a point $y$ of $Y$ over a field, the étale cohomology of the fibre in degrees at most $1$ is a *symplectic pair* $H^{\le 1}_{\text{ét}}(X_{\bar y}, \mathbb{Q}_p) = (H^0, H^1)$ with Galois action. Here $H^0$ is the $\mathbb{Q}_p$-algebra of functions on the geometric points of the fibre of $Y'$. The polarization makes $H^1$ an $H^0$-module with a Galois-equivariant perfect alternating pairing to $H^0(-1)$ [@bettsstix2025, §4.1, Lemma 4.2 and Definition 4.3, pp. 32–33, and Remark 6.6, p. 46].

Fix a finite place $v$ of $K$ above $p$, and a finite set $S$ of places containing those above $p$ and $\infty$ and those where the family has bad reduction. Such an $S$ is called *suitable*. A symplectic pair $(A, V)$ of $G_K$-representations is *$S$-good* if:

- $A$ is unramified outside $S$;
- $V$ is unramified, pure and integral of weight $1$ outside $S$;
- $V$ is de Rham at the places above $p$, with Hodge–Tate weights in $\{0, 1\}$.

The *Lawrence–Venkatesh locus* $Y(K_v)^{\mathrm{LV}}_{X,S}$ is the set of $y_v \in Y(K_v)$ for which $H^{\le 1}_{\text{ét}}(X_{\bar y_v}, \mathbb{Q}_p)$ is isomorphic, as a symplectic pair of $G_v$-representations, to the restriction of an $S$-good pair [@bettsstix2025, Definition 6.2, pp. 43–44].

**Proof of Theorem A.** It has two halves [@bettsstix2025, §1.1, p. 3].

1. *Selmer sections land in the locus.* For every abelian-by-finite family and every suitable $S$, the image of $\mathrm{loc}_v$ lies in $Y(K_v)^{\mathrm{LV}}_{X,S}$ [@bettsstix2025, Theorem 7.1, p. 54, and Lemma 6.4, p. 44].
   - The relative cohomology sheaves of the family are $\mathbb{Q}_p$-local systems on $Y$, that is, representations of $\pi_1(Y)$. Restricting them along a section $s$ gives a symplectic pair $(A_s, V_s)$ of $G_K$-representations, whose isomorphism class depends only on the conjugacy class of $s$ [@bettsstix2025, §7.2, p. 56].
   - If $s|_{G_u}$ is the section of $y_u \in Y(K_u)$, then $(A_s, V_s)|_{G_u} \cong H^{\le 1}_{\text{ét}}(X_{\bar y_u}, \mathbb{Q}_p)$, because the local section is induced by $y_u$ as a morphism of pointed schemes [@bettsstix2025, Proposition 7.2 and its proof, p. 56].
   - For a Selmer section, choose for every place $u$ a point $y_u \in Y(K_u)$ whose section is $s|_{G_u}$. At a finite place, $y_u = \mathrm{loc}_u(s)$; at an infinite place, $y_u$ need not be unique. Then $(A_s, V_s)$ is a single global pair interpolating the chosen local points $y_u$ at every place [@bettsstix2025, Definition 6.1, p. 43, and proof of Theorem 7.1, p. 56].
   - Any such interpolating pair is $S$-good. At places outside $S$ the fibres have good reduction, so their cohomology is unramified, pure and integral. Above $p$ it is de Rham with the right Hodge–Tate weights [@bettsstix2025, Lemma 6.4 and its proof, p. 44].
2. *The locus is finite.* If $v$ is self-conjugate, defined below, then some abelian-by-finite family has $Y(K_v)^{\mathrm{LV}}_{X,S}$ finite for every suitable $S$ [@bettsstix2025, Theorem 6.5, p. 44].

If $K$ contains no CM subfield, every finite place is self-conjugate, so the two halves give Theorem A [@bettsstix2025, p. 3, and Remark 2.18 (1), p. 13].

**Self-conjugate places.** Two finite places $v, v'$ of $K$ are *conjugate* if, for every CM subfield $E$ of $K$, complex conjugation on $E$ carries the restriction of $v$ to $E$ to that of $v'$. A place is *self-conjugate* if it is conjugate to itself [@bettsstix2025, Definition 2.17, p. 13]. If $K$ has a CM subfield, let $E$ be its maximal CM subfield. Then $v$ is self-conjugate exactly when its restriction to $E$ is fixed by complex conjugation. Lawrence and Venkatesh's *friendly* places are exactly the self-conjugate places unramified over $\mathbb{Q}$ [@bettsstix2025, Remark 2.18, p. 13].

The condition is used for its numerical consequence. Let $V$ be a representation of $G_K$ that is de Rham above $p$, and unramified and pure of weight $n$ outside a finite set. If $v$ is self-conjugate, the average Hodge–Tate weight of $V$ at $v$ is $n/2$ [@bettsstix2025, Proposition 2.19 and Corollary 2.21, pp. 13–14].

**Finiteness of the locus.**

- *Period maps.* For $y_0 \in Y(K_v)$ and a small neighbourhood $U_{y_0}$ of $y_0$ in the rigid analytic space, the $v$-adic period map $\Phi_{y_0}$ goes from $U_{y_0}$ to the Grassmannian $\mathcal{H}_{y_0}$ of Lagrangian $H^0_{\mathrm{dR}}(X_{y_0}/K_v)$-submodules of $H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(X_{y_0}/K_v)$. It sends $y$ to the Hodge filtration of the fibre at $y$, transported to $y_0$ by the Gauss–Manin connection. The main technical result is that $\Phi_{y_0}(y)$ determines the filtered $(\varphi, N, G_v)$-module of $H^1_{\text{ét}}(X_{\bar y}, \mathbb{Q}_p)$ [@bettsstix2025, §1.1.2, p. 4].
  - Lawrence and Venkatesh needed this only for families with good reduction at $v$, where crystalline cohomology suffices.
  - Betts and Stix prove it in general, with Scholze's relative $p$-adic Hodge theory and Shimizu's $p$-adic monodromy theorem [@bettsstix2025, Remark 1.6, p. 4].
- *Full monodromy.* If the family has full monodromy, a topological condition, then $\Phi_{y_0}$ has Zariski-dense image. So on a closed disc, a set of points whose image is not Zariski-dense is finite [@bettsstix2025, Lemma 5.9 and Corollary 5.10, p. 43]. Since $Y(K_v)$ is covered by finitely many such discs, it suffices to show that the image of the locus is not Zariski-dense [@bettsstix2025, Remark 5.11, p. 43]. Lawrence and Venkatesh verified full monodromy for their Kodaira–Parshin families [@bettsstix2025, §1.1.3, p. 5].
- *The principal trichotomy.* Let $(A, V)$ be a symplectic pair of $G_K$-representations in which $A \cong \mathbb{Q}_p^n$ as a $\mathbb{Q}_p$-algebra, so that $G_K$ acts on $A$ by permuting the factors, and $V$ has constant rank $2d > 0$ over $A$. Suppose that $V$ is de Rham above $p$ with Hodge–Tate weights in $\{0, 1\}$ at $v$, and unramified and pure of weight $1$ outside a finite set. Let $\Sigma$ be the set of $\mathbb{Q}_p$-algebra maps $\psi : A \to \mathbb{Q}_p$, with stabilizers $G_\psi \le G_K$ and $G_{w_\psi} = G_\psi \cap G_v$, and let $V_\psi = \mathbb{Q}_p \otimes_{A,\psi} V$. If $v$ is self-conjugate, then at least one of the following holds [@bettsstix2025, Proposition 6.7, p. 46]:
  - (a) for some $\psi$ with $[G_v : G_{w_\psi}] \ge 4$, $V_\psi$ is GSp-irreducible as a representation of $G_\psi$;
  - (b) for some $\psi$ with $[G_v : G_{w_\psi}] \ge 4$, $V_\psi$ has a nonzero isotropic $G_\psi$-subrepresentation whose average Hodge–Tate weight at $w_\psi$ is at least $1/2$;
  - (c) at least $\dim_{\mathbb{Q}_p}(A)/(d+1)$ of the $\psi$ have $[G_v : G_{w_\psi}] < 4$.

  The proof assumes that (a) and (b) fail, sums the resulting Hodge–Tate inequalities over the $G_K$-orbit of $\psi$, and compares the sum with the average-weight formula at self-conjugate places [@bettsstix2025, proof of Proposition 6.7, pp. 46–47].
  - *The split hypothesis.* Proposition 6.7 is stated for any symplectic pair, but its proof first reduces to $A = \prod_{\psi \in \Sigma} \mathbb{Q}_p$ [@bettsstix2025, proof of Proposition 6.7, p. 46], and that reduction fails for nonreduced $A$. For example, take $p = 3$, $K = \mathbb{Q}$ and $v = 3$, let $A = \mathbb{Q}_3[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^3)$ with trivial Galois action, and let $V = A \otimes_{\mathbb{Q}_3} H^1_{\text{ét}}(E_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}, \mathbb{Q}_3)$ for an elliptic curve $E/\mathbb{Q}$, with the Weil pairing extended $A$-bilinearly. Then $d = 1$ and $\Sigma$ has one element, of index $1$. So (a) and (b) fail, and (c) asks for at least $3/2$ elements.
  - The application needs only split $A$. The pair of a $K_v$-point has $H^0 = \prod_{Y'_{y_v}(\bar K_v)} \mathbb{Q}_p$, and $\Sigma$ is identified with the geometric points of the fibre of $Y'$ [@bettsstix2025, Remark 6.6, p. 46]. So any pair interpolating a point of $Y(K_v)$ has $A \cong \mathbb{Q}_p^n$ as an algebra [@bettsstix2025, Remark 6.3, p. 44].
- *Types.* Definition 6.8 sorts pairs by these conditions, with (b) weakened: a pair of $G_v$-representations is of *type (b)* if some $\psi$ with $[G_v : G_{w_\psi}] \ge 4$ has a nonzero isotropic $G_{w_\psi}$-subrepresentation $W \le V_\psi$ of average Hodge–Tate weight at least $1/2$. Since $G_{w_\psi} \le G_\psi$, a global subrepresentation as in (b) is in particular local. Hence, for a family of constant relative dimension $d > 0$ and a self-conjugate $v$, every point of $Y(K_v)^{\mathrm{LV}}_{X,S}$ satisfies one of these: it is interpolated by an $S$-good pair of type (a); its own pair is of type (b); or its $H^0$ is of type (c) [@bettsstix2025, Definition 6.8, p. 47].
- *Types (a) and (b).* For a family with full monodromy, the points interpolated by an $S$-good pair of type (a) form a finite set, and so do the points whose pair is of type (b) [@bettsstix2025, Corollaries 6.10 and 6.16, pp. 48 and 50]. For (a), the Hermite–Minkowski theorem and Faltings' lemma leave finitely many possibilities. Each confines the period image to a subvariety of dimension at most $d_i(2d_i + 1)$. Here $d_i$ is the dimension of the relevant abelian variety, over a field $L_{w_i}$ of degree at least $4$ over $K_v$. The Weil-restricted Grassmannian has larger dimension $[L_{w_i} : K_v] \cdot d_i(d_i+1)/2$ [@bettsstix2025, Lemma 6.13 and its proof, pp. 48–49]. For (b), the input is a lemma on four Lagrangian subspaces of a symplectic space [@bettsstix2025, Sublemma 6.18, p. 51].
- *Type (c) and the Kodaira–Parshin family.* For an odd prime $q$, Lawrence and Venkatesh's Kodaira–Parshin family $X_q \to Y'_q \to Y$ has relative dimension $(g - 1/2)(q-1)$ [@bettsstix2025, §6.4, p. 52]. Let $q_v$ be the size of the residue field of $K_v$, and $n_v$ the number of extensions of $K_v$ of degree $2$ or $3$ inside $\bar K_v$. Suppose that:
  - $q - 1$ is divisible neither by $4$ nor by any odd prime divisor of $q_v(q_v+1)(q_v^3-1)$;
  - $q - 1 \ge 9 n_v$.

  Then no point is of type (c) for $X_q$ [@bettsstix2025, Proposition 6.20, p. 52]. The proof counts surjections from the geometric fundamental group onto $\mathbb{Z}/k$, with $k = (q-1)/2$. The first condition makes the Weil pairing vanish on the invariants of subgroups of index $2$ or $3$ in $G_v$, which bounds the proportion of points of the fibre of $Y'_q$ whose $G_v$-orbits have fewer than $4$ elements [@bettsstix2025, proof of Proposition 6.20, pp. 52–53]. If $v \nmid 6$, then $n_v = 7$ [@bettsstix2025, Remark 6.21, p. 53]. Dirichlet's theorem gives such a $q$ for every finite place $v$ [@bettsstix2025, Corollary 6.22, p. 53].

**Comparison with Lawrence–Venkatesh.**

- *Places covered.* In these terms, Lawrence and Venkatesh essentially prove two things: $Y(K) \subseteq Y(K_v)^{\mathrm{LV}}_{X,S}$, and finiteness of the locus for some place $v$ above $p$ and some odd prime $q$. With half 1, this gives Theorem A at one place [@bettsstix2025, Remark 1.3, p. 3]. With careful bookkeeping it gives a set of places of natural density $1$, when $K$ has no CM subfield [@bettsstix2025, Remark 6.23 (I), p. 54].
- *Places left over.* The original method never covers places ramified over $\mathbb{Q}$, places above $2$, or places of bad reduction of $Y$. Extending it to those places is most of the work of the paper [@bettsstix2025, Remark 1.3, p. 3, and Remark 6.23 (II), p. 54].
- *Smaller families.* Keeping track of the symplectic pairings also shrinks the families needed. For a curve of genus $3$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, the original argument proves finiteness of $Y(\mathbb{Q})$ with the Kodaira–Parshin family for $q \ge 23$, of relative dimension at least $55$. The symplectic version works with $q = 11$, of relative dimension $25$ [@bettsstix2025, Remark 1.4, p. 3].

**Fields with a CM subfield (checked here).** The two halves are stated for every number field $K$, so Theorem A holds at every self-conjugate finite place $v$ of every number field.

- Half 1 puts the image of $\mathrm{loc}_v$ inside $Y(K_v)^{\mathrm{LV}}_{X,S}$ for every family and every suitable $S$.
- Half 2 gives a family whose locus is finite for every suitable $S$.
- A suitable $S$ exists for each family. Spreading out the family over a smooth proper model of $Y$ over a dense open subscheme of $\mathrm{Spec}\, \mathcal{O}_K$ shows that it has good reduction at all but finitely many places.

For example, $K = \mathbb{Q}(i)$ is its own maximal CM subfield. Its self-conjugate places are the place above $2$ and the places above primes $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. Complex conjugation interchanges the two places above each prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, and at those places the argument says nothing. Whether later work covers places that are not self-conjugate has not been checked.
