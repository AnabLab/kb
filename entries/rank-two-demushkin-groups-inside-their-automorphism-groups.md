**Setting.** Let $p$ be a prime. Write an element of the affine group $\mathbb{Q}_p \rtimes \mathbb{Q}_p^\times$ as a pair $(b, \gamma)$, standing for the map $x \mapsto \gamma x + b$. Then
$$(b, \gamma)(b', \gamma') = (b + \gamma b', \gamma\gamma'), \qquad (b, \gamma)^{-1} = (-\gamma^{-1}b, \gamma^{-1}), \qquad (b, \gamma)(a, c)(b, \gamma)^{-1} = (\gamma a + (1 - c)b,\ c).$$
Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{Z}_p^\times$ be an infinite closed subgroup that is procyclic and pro-$p$. Then $U \cong \mathbb{Z}_p$. Choose a topological generator $u$ of $U$ and put $m = v_p(1 - u)$. Here $m \ge 1$: for odd $p$, $U$ lies in $1 + p\mathbb{Z}_p$, because its image in $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$ is a $p$-group in a group of order $p - 1$; and for $p = 2$, $u$ is odd. Step 1 shows that $m$ does not depend on the choice of $u$. Put
$$D_U = \{(a, c) : a \in \mathbb{Z}_p,\ c \in U\} = \mathbb{Z}_p \rtimes U, \qquad G_p = \{(b, \gamma) : b \in \mathbb{Z}_p,\ \gamma \in \mathbb{Z}_p^\times\} = \mathbb{Z}_p \rtimes \mathbb{Z}_p^\times,$$
with the topology from $\mathbb{Q}_p \times \mathbb{Q}_p^\times$. These are profinite groups, and $D_U$ is pro-$p$. Let $T = \{(a, 1)\} \subseteq D_U$ and $T_G = \{(b, 1)\} \subseteq G_p$ be the translation subgroups.

For a centre-free profinite group $D$, identify $d \in D$ with the inner automorphism $c_d(x) = dxd^{-1}$, as in [the problem](demushkin-groups-inside-their-automorphism-groups.md). Automorphisms are continuous, and $\mathrm{Aut}(D)$ carries the topology defined by the characteristic open subgroups of $D$.

**Theorem.** This is proved here.

1. *The groups.*
   - (a) A Demushkin group of rank $2$ has trivial centre if and only if it is isomorphic to some $D_U$.
   - (b) Each $D_U$ is a Demushkin group of rank $2$ with $q = p^m$. For odd $p$, $U = 1 + p^m\mathbb{Z}_p$.
2. *Automorphisms.* $D_U$ is centre-free. There is an isomorphism of profinite groups $\mathrm{Aut}(D_U) \to G_p$ that maps $D_U = \mathrm{Inn}(D_U)$ onto $D'_U = \{(p^m a, c) : a \in \mathbb{Z}_p,\ c \in U\}$. In particular, $\mathrm{Aut}(D_U)$ does not depend on $U$.
3. *Odd $p$.* Every continuous automorphism of $G_p$ is inner, and $G_p$ is centre-free. So $D_U$ is characteristic in $\mathrm{Aut}(D_U)$, and $\mathrm{Out}(\mathrm{Aut}(D_U)) = 1$. But the closed normal subgroups of $G_p$ isomorphic to $D_U$ are exactly $N_j = \{(p^j a, c) : a \in \mathbb{Z}_p,\ c \in U\}$ for $0 \le j \le m$. There are $m + 1 \ge 2$ of them, and $N_0$ contains $D'_U = N_m$ with index $p^m$.
4. *$p = 2$.* Let $\varepsilon$ be conjugation by $(\tfrac12, 1)$ in $\mathbb{Q}_2 \rtimes \mathbb{Q}_2^\times$, so $\varepsilon(b, \gamma) = (b + \tfrac{1 - \gamma}{2}, \gamma)$. Then $\varepsilon$ is an automorphism of $G_2$ that is not inner, every continuous automorphism of $G_2$ is inner or $\varepsilon$ followed by an inner one, and $\varepsilon(D'_U) \ne D'_U$. So $D_U$ is not characteristic in $\mathrm{Aut}(D_U)$, and $\mathrm{Out}(\mathrm{Aut}(D_U)) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$.

So for the centre-free Demushkin groups of rank $2$, the questions of [the problem](demushkin-groups-inside-their-automorphism-groups.md) have these answers. For odd $p$, $D$ is characteristic in $\mathrm{Aut}(D)$ and $\mathrm{Out}(\mathrm{Aut}(D)) = 1$, but $D$ is not the only closed normal subgroup isomorphic to $D$. For $p = 2$, $D$ is not characteristic, and $\mathrm{Out}(\mathrm{Aut}(D)) \ne 1$.

**Corollary ($\ell$-adic fields).** This is proved here. Let $\ell \ne p$ be a prime, let $K/\mathbb{Q}_\ell$ be finite with residue field of order $N$, and let $G_K(p)$ be the Galois group of the maximal $p$-extension of $K$. Suppose $N \equiv 1 \pmod p$, which always holds for $p = 2$. Then $G_K(p) \cong D_U$ with $U$ the closed subgroup generated by $N$, and $m = v_p(N - 1)$. So parts 3 and 4 apply. For example, $G_{\mathbb{Q}_3}(2)$ is a Demushkin group that is not characteristic in its automorphism group, and $G_{\mathbb{Q}_7}(3)$ is characteristic in its automorphism group but is one of two closed normal copies of itself there.

The condition $N \equiv 1 \pmod p$ means that $\zeta_p \in K$: since $x^p - 1$ is separable modulo the maximal ideal, Hensel's lemma lifts its roots from the residue field.

**Proof.**

*Step 0: a presentation.* For $w \in \mathbb{Z}_p^\times$, with $w \in 1 + p\mathbb{Z}_p$ if $p$ is odd, let $H_w$ be the pro-$p$ group with generators $x, y$ and the single relation $yxy^{-1} = x^w$, that is, the quotient of the free pro-$p$ group on $x, y$ by the closed normal subgroup generated by $yxy^{-1}x^{-w}$.

- If $w = 1$, then $H_w$ is abelian, and it is nontrivial since it maps onto $\mathbb{Z}_p$. If $p = 2$ and $w = -1$, then $y^2$ is central, since $y^2xy^{-2} = yx^{-1}y^{-1} = x$, and $y^2 \ne 1$, since $x \mapsto 0$, $y \mapsto 1$ defines a homomorphism onto $\mathbb{Z}_2$. In both cases the centre of $H_w$ is nontrivial.
- If $w$ has infinite order, let $U = \overline{\langle w \rangle}$. Then $x \mapsto (1, 1)$, $y \mapsto (0, w)$ defines an isomorphism $h : H_w \to D_U$. Indeed, $(0, w)(1, 1)(0, w)^{-1} = (w, 1) = (1, 1)^w$, so $h$ exists. Its image is closed and contains the closures of $\langle (1, 1) \rangle$ and $\langle (0, w) \rangle$, which are $T$ and $\{0\} \times U$, so $h$ is onto. Let $C$ be the closure of $\langle x \rangle$. Conjugation by $y$ maps $C$ onto the closure of $\langle x^w \rangle$, which is $C$ as $w$ is a unit. So $C$ is normalized by $x$ and $y$, hence by $H_w$, because normalizers of closed subgroups are closed. The map $\mathbb{Z}_p \to C$, $k \mapsto x^k$, is onto, and its composite with $h$ is $k \mapsto (k, 1)$, which is injective. So $h$ maps $C$ isomorphically onto $T$, and induces $H_w/C \to D_U/T$. Similarly $k \mapsto y^kC$ maps $\mathbb{Z}_p$ onto the procyclic group $H_w/C$, and its composite with $H_w/C \to D_U/T \cong U$ is $k \mapsto w^k$. Its kernel is a closed subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}_p$, so it is $0$ or some $p^j\mathbb{Z}_p$, and the latter would give $w^{p^j} = 1$. So $H_w/C \to D_U/T$ is injective. Hence $h$ is injective, and a continuous bijection of profinite groups is an isomorphism.

*Step 1: the group $D_U$.* Every $c \in U$ satisfies $c \equiv 1 \pmod{p^m}$, because the kernel of $\mathbb{Z}_p^\times \to (\mathbb{Z}_p/p^m)^\times$ is a closed subgroup containing $u$. So $T_m = \{(a, 1) : a \in p^m\mathbb{Z}_p\}$ is normal in $D_U$, and for $g = (a, c)$, $g' = (a', c')$ in $D_U$ the products $gg'$ and $g'g$ have the same second coordinate and first coordinates differing by $(c - 1)a' - (c' - 1)a \in p^m\mathbb{Z}_p$. So $gg'(g'g)^{-1} \in T_m$, and the closed commutator subgroup lies in $T_m$. It contains $(0, u)(1, 1)(0, u)^{-1}(1, 1)^{-1} = (u - 1, 1)$, which generates $T_m$ topologically. So the closed commutator subgroup is $T_m$, and $D_U^{\mathrm{ab}} = D_U/T_m$ contains $T/T_m \cong \mathbb{Z}/p^m$ with torsion-free quotient $D_U/T \cong U \cong \mathbb{Z}_p$. Hence $T/T_m$ is the torsion subgroup of $D_U^{\mathrm{ab}}$. So $m$ depends only on $U$, and $q(D_U) = p^m$, since $q$ is the order of the torsion subgroup of the abelianization. Since $T$ is the preimage of this torsion subgroup, $T$ is characteristic in $D_U$.

$D_U$ is centre-free. If $(a, c)$ is central, then comparing $(a, c)(1, 1) = (a + c, c)$ with $(1, 1)(a, c) = (1 + a, c)$ gives $c = 1$, and comparing $(a, 1)(0, u) = (a, u)$ with $(0, u)(a, 1) = (ua, u)$ gives $a = 0$.

*Step 2: part 1.* (a) Let $D$ be a Demushkin group of rank $2$. If $q \ne 2$, then $D$ is given by generators $x_1, x_2$ and the relation $x_1^q(x_1, x_2) = 1$, where $(x, y) = xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$ [@serre1963, Théorème 3.1 and Remarque a, p. 147]. Here $q = p^f$ with $f \ge 1$, or $q = 0$ when $D^{\mathrm{ab}} \cong \mathbb{Z}_p^2$ [@serre1963, §3 b, p. 146]. The relation says $x_2x_1x_2^{-1} = x_1^{1+q}$, so $D \cong H_{1+q}$. If $q = 2$, then $D$ is given by generators $x, y$ and a relation $yxy^{-1} = x^{-(1+k)}$ with $k = 0$ or $k = 2^s$, $s \ge 2$ [@serre1963, paragraph after Théorème 3.2, p. 147], so $D \cong H_{-(1+k)}$. In each case $D \cong H_w$ with $w = 1$, $w = -1$ (and $p = 2$), or $w$ of infinite order: $1 + q$ for $q \ge 3$ lies in the torsion-free group $1 + p\mathbb{Z}_p$, or in $1 + 4\mathbb{Z}_2$ when $p = 2$, and is not $1$, and the square of $-(1 + 2^s)$ lies in $1 + 4\mathbb{Z}_2$ and is not $1$. By Step 0, $D$ is centre-free exactly when $w$ has infinite order, and then $D \cong D_U$ with $U = \overline{\langle w \rangle}$. Conversely, $D_U$ is centre-free by Step 1.

(b) Let $q = p^m$. With the convention $(a, b) = a^{-1}b^{-1}ab$, the [classification](classification-of-demushkin-groups.md) shows that the following relations define Demushkin groups of rank $2$: $x_1^q(x_1, x_2)$ if $q \ne 2$ (normal form 1), and $x_1^{2+2^f}(x_1, x_2)$ for $f \ge 2$ (normal form 3, first case). With $y = x_2^{-1}$ these relations read $yx_1y^{-1} = x_1^{1-q}$ and $yx_1y^{-1} = x_1^{-1-2^f}$, so these groups are $H_{1-q}$ and $H_{-1-2^f}$, which are $D_{U'}$ for $U' = \overline{\langle 1 - q \rangle}$ and $U' = \overline{\langle -1-2^f \rangle}$ by Step 0. It remains to see that $U$ is of one of these forms.

- If $p$ is odd, or $p = 2$ and $U \subseteq 1 + 4\mathbb{Z}_2$, then $m \ge 2$ when $p = 2$, so $q \ne 2$. The group $1 + p^m\mathbb{Z}_p$ is procyclic, topologically generated by any of its elements not in $1 + p^{m+1}\mathbb{Z}_p$. By Step 1, $u$ is such an element, and so is $1 - q$. So $U = 1 + p^m\mathbb{Z}_p = \overline{\langle 1 - q \rangle}$. For odd $p$, $U \subseteq 1 + p\mathbb{Z}_p$ always, which gives the last claim of (b).
- If $p = 2$ and $U \not\subseteq 1 + 4\mathbb{Z}_2$, then $u \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, so $m = 1$, and $f = v_2(1 + u)$ satisfies $2 \le f < \infty$, since $u \ne -1$. Let $\rho : \mathbb{Z}_2^\times \to 1 + 4\mathbb{Z}_2$ send $c$ to whichever of $\pm c$ is $1 \bmod 4$. It is a continuous homomorphism with kernel $\{\pm 1\}$, and it is injective on the torsion-free group $U$. Its image $\rho(U)$ is the closure of $\langle -u \rangle$, which is $1 + 2^f\mathbb{Z}_2$. So $U = \{\epsilon(z)z : z \in 1 + 2^f\mathbb{Z}_2\}$ for a continuous homomorphism $\epsilon : 1 + 2^f\mathbb{Z}_2 \to \{\pm 1\}$ with $\epsilon(-u) = -1$. The group $1 + 2^f\mathbb{Z}_2 \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$ has only one nontrivial such homomorphism. The same description holds for $U' = \overline{\langle -1-2^f \rangle}$, with $1 + 2^f$ in place of $-u$, so $U = U'$.


*Step 3: part 2.* Let $\sigma$ be an automorphism of $D_U$. By Step 1, $\sigma(T) = T$, and $\sigma$ acts on $T \cong \mathbb{Z}_p$ as multiplication by some $\gamma \in \mathbb{Z}_p^\times$. Write $\sigma(0, c) = (\tau(c), \varphi(c))$. Applying $\sigma$ to $(0, c)(a, 1)(0, c)^{-1} = (ca, 1)$ gives $(\varphi(c)\gamma a, 1) = (\gamma ca, 1)$, so $\varphi(c) = c$. Applying it to $(0, c)(0, c') = (0, cc')$ gives
$$\tau(cc') = \tau(c) + c\,\tau(c'),$$
and $\tau$ is continuous. So $\sigma$ is determined by $\gamma$ and $\tau(u)$, because $D_U$ is topologically generated by $(1, 1)$ and $(0, u)$.

Let $\hat A = \{(b, \gamma) : b \in p^{-m}\mathbb{Z}_p,\ \gamma \in \mathbb{Z}_p^\times\}$, a compact subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}_p \rtimes \mathbb{Q}_p^\times$. By the conjugation formula, $(b, \gamma) \in \hat A$ maps $(a, c) \in D_U$ to $(\gamma a + (1 - c)b, c)$, which lies in $D_U$ because $1 - c \in p^m\mathbb{Z}_p$ by Step 1. This gives a homomorphism $\kappa : \hat A \to \mathrm{Aut}(D_U)$, whose restriction to $D_U \subseteq \hat A$ is $d \mapsto c_d$.

- $\kappa$ is injective: if $\kappa(b, \gamma) = 1$, then $\gamma = 1$ from $(1, 1)$, and $(1 - u)b = 0$ from $(0, u)$, so $b = 0$.
- $\kappa$ is onto: given $\sigma$ as above, put $b = \tau(u)/(1 - u) \in p^{-m}\mathbb{Z}_p$. Then $\kappa(b, \gamma)$ agrees with $\sigma$ on $(1, 1)$ and $(0, u)$, so they are equal.
- $\kappa$ is continuous: for a characteristic open subgroup $V$ of $D_U$, the $(b, \gamma)$ with $\kappa(b, \gamma)$ trivial on $D_U/V$ are those with $(\gamma, 1) \in (1, 1)V$ and $((1 - u)b, u) \in (0, u)V$, an open condition.

So $\kappa$ is an isomorphism of profinite groups. Conjugation by $(0, p^m)$ in $\mathbb{Q}_p \rtimes \mathbb{Q}_p^\times$ maps $(b, \gamma)$ to $(p^mb, \gamma)$, so it carries $\hat A$ onto $G_p$ and $D_U$ onto $D'_U$. The composite of $\kappa^{-1}$ with it is the isomorphism of part 2.

*Step 4: automorphisms of $G_p$.* Every abelian normal subgroup $B$ of $G_p$ lies in $T_G$. Indeed, suppose $(b, \gamma) \in B$ with $\gamma \ne 1$. Conjugating it by $(1, 1)$ gives $(b + 1 - \gamma, \gamma) \in B$, and $(b + 1 - \gamma, \gamma)(b, \gamma)^{-1} = (1 - \gamma, 1) \in B$. But $(b, \gamma)(1 - \gamma, 1) = (b + \gamma(1 - \gamma), \gamma)$ and $(1 - \gamma, 1)(b, \gamma) = (b + 1 - \gamma, \gamma)$ differ, as $\gamma \ne 1$, which contradicts $B$ being abelian. So $T_G$ is the largest abelian normal subgroup of $G_p$, and every automorphism preserves it. The argument of Step 3, with $\mathbb{Z}_p^\times$ in place of $U$, shows that a continuous automorphism $\sigma$ of $G_p$ has the form $\sigma(t, 1) = (\gamma t, 1)$ and $\sigma(0, c) = (\tau(c), c)$, with $\gamma \in \mathbb{Z}_p^\times$ and $\tau(cc') = \tau(c) + c\tau(c')$ for $c, c' \in \mathbb{Z}_p^\times$. As $\mathbb{Z}_p^\times$ is commutative, $\tau(c) + c\tau(c') = \tau(c') + c'\tau(c)$, that is, $(1 - c')\tau(c) = (1 - c)\tau(c')$. Taking $c' = -1$ gives
$$\tau(c) = (1 - c)\beta \quad \text{for all } c \in \mathbb{Z}_p^\times, \qquad \beta = \tau(-1)/2.$$
Conjugation by $(\beta, \gamma)$ maps $(t, 1)$ to $(\gamma t, 1)$ and $(0, c)$ to $((1 - c)\beta, c)$, so $\sigma$ is conjugation by $(\beta, \gamma) \in \mathbb{Q}_p \rtimes \mathbb{Q}_p^\times$.

An element $(b, \gamma)$ of $\mathbb{Q}_p \rtimes \mathbb{Q}_p^\times$ that commutes with every element of $G_p$ is trivial: comparing $(b, \gamma)(1, 1)$ with $(1, 1)(b, \gamma)$ gives $\gamma = 1$, and comparing $(b, 1)(0, -1) = (b, -1)$ with $(0, -1)(b, 1) = (-b, -1)$ gives $b = -b$, so $b = 0$. In particular $G_p$ is centre-free.

- *Odd $p$.* Then $\beta \in \mathbb{Z}_p$, so $\sigma$ is inner. With centre-freeness, this is the first claim of part 3. By part 2, every continuous automorphism of $\mathrm{Aut}(D_U)$ is inner, so it preserves the normal subgroup $\mathrm{Inn}(D_U)$, and $\mathrm{Out}(\mathrm{Aut}(D_U)) = 1$.
- *$p = 2$.* Then $\beta \in \tfrac12\mathbb{Z}_2$. Conversely, for $\beta \in \tfrac12\mathbb{Z}_2$ and $\gamma \in \mathbb{Z}_2^\times$, conjugation by $(\beta, \gamma)$ maps $(t, c) \in G_2$ to $(\gamma t + (1 - c)\beta, c) \in G_2$, because every $c \in \mathbb{Z}_2^\times$ is odd. So the continuous automorphisms of $G_2$ are the conjugations by elements of $\{(\beta, \gamma) : \beta \in \tfrac12\mathbb{Z}_2,\ \gamma \in \mathbb{Z}_2^\times\}$, and distinct elements give distinct automorphisms by the centralizer computation. The inner ones come from $G_2$, a subgroup of index $2$. So $\mathrm{Out}(G_2) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$, generated by the class of $\varepsilon$. Finally, $\varepsilon(0, u) = (\tfrac{1 - u}{2}, u)$ has first coordinate of valuation $m - 1$, while every element of $D'_U$ with second coordinate $u$ has first coordinate in $2^m\mathbb{Z}_2$. So $\varepsilon(D'_U) \ne D'_U$, and by part 2, $D_U$ is not characteristic in $\mathrm{Aut}(D_U)$ and $\mathrm{Out}(\mathrm{Aut}(D_U)) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$.

*Step 5: normal copies for odd $p$.* Let $p$ be odd, so $U = 1 + p^m\mathbb{Z}_p$. For $0 \le j \le k$ with $k \ge 1$, put $N_{j,k} = \{(a, c) : a \in p^j\mathbb{Z}_p,\ c \in 1 + p^k\mathbb{Z}_p\}$. It is a closed subgroup, and it is normal in $G_p$ by the conjugation formula, since $(1 - c)b \in p^k\mathbb{Z}_p \subseteq p^j\mathbb{Z}_p$. Conjugation by $(0, p^{-j})$ maps it isomorphically onto $D_{1 + p^k\mathbb{Z}_p}$, which has $q = p^k$ by Step 1. As $q$ is an isomorphism invariant, $N_{j,k} \cong D_U$ exactly when $k = m$, and $N_{j,m} = N_j$.

Conversely, let $N \subseteq G_p$ be a closed normal subgroup isomorphic to $D_U$. Its image under $(b, \gamma) \mapsto \gamma$ is a closed pro-$p$ subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}_p^\times$, so it lies in $1 + p\mathbb{Z}_p$ and is $1 + p^k\mathbb{Z}_p$ for some $k \ge 1$, or trivial. The intersection $N \cap T_G$ is a closed subgroup of $T_G \cong \mathbb{Z}_p$, so it is $\{(a, 1) : a \in p^j\mathbb{Z}_p\}$ for some $j \ge 0$, or trivial. If either were trivial, $N$ would be abelian, but $D_U$ is not, by Step 1. Choose $(b_0, u_0) \in N$ with $u_0 = 1 + p^k$. Conjugating by $(t, 1)$ and dividing by $(b_0, u_0)$ gives $((1 - u_0)t, 1) \in N$ for all $t \in \mathbb{Z}_p$, so $p^k\mathbb{Z}_p \subseteq p^j\mathbb{Z}_p$ and $j \le k$. For any $(b, c) \in N$, conjugating by $(0, -1)$ gives $(-b, c) \in N$, and $(-b, c)(b, c)^{-1} = (-2b, 1) \in N$, so $b \in p^j\mathbb{Z}_p$ as $2$ is a unit. Hence $N \subseteq N_{j,k}$. Conversely, $N$ contains the translations by $p^j\mathbb{Z}_p$, and for each $c \in 1 + p^k\mathbb{Z}_p$ some $(b, c)$ with $b \in p^j\mathbb{Z}_p$, hence $(-b, 1)(b, c) = (0, c)$. So $N = N_{j,k}$, and $k = m$. This proves the rest of part 3. $\square$

*Proof of the corollary.* Let $K_{\mathrm{tr}}$ be the maximal tamely ramified extension of $K$. The group $\mathrm{Gal}(K_{\mathrm{tr}}/K)$ is isomorphic to the profinite group topologically generated by two elements $\tau, \sigma$ with the only relation $\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1} = \tau^N$, where $\tau$ generates the inertia group and $\sigma$ is a Frobenius lift [@luo2024, Proposition 2.15 (i), p. 9]. A finite Galois extension of $K$ of $p$-power degree has ramification index prime to $\ell$, so it is tamely ramified and lies in $K_{\mathrm{tr}}$. Hence $G_K(p)$ is the maximal pro-$p$ quotient of $\mathrm{Gal}(K_{\mathrm{tr}}/K)$. By the universal properties of the two presentations, this quotient is the pro-$p$ group with generators $\tau, \sigma$ and the relation $\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1} = \tau^N$, which is $H_N$ with $x = \tau$, $y = \sigma$. Here $N \in 1 + p\mathbb{Z}_p$ if $p$ is odd, and $N$ has infinite order in $\mathbb{Z}_p^\times$, since $N^k > 1$ for integers $k \ge 1$. By Step 0, $G_K(p) \cong D_U$ with $U = \overline{\langle N \rangle}$, and $m = v_p(1 - N)$. By part 1(b), it is a Demushkin group of rank $2$. For $\mathbb{Q}_3$ and $p = 2$, $N = 3$ and $m = 1$; for $\mathbb{Q}_7$ and $p = 3$, $N = 7$ and $m = 1$, so there are $m + 1 = 2$ normal copies. $\square$

Luo states the case of completions of number fields directly [@luo2024, Theorem 2.19 (i), p. 10].

**Remarks.**

- *Scope.* The Demushkin groups of rank $2$ with nontrivial centre are $\mathbb{Z}_p^2$, where $q = 0$, and, for $p = 2$, the group with relation $yxy^{-1} = x^{-1}$, where $q = 2$. They are excluded from the question. The centre-free ones are nonabelian, so they fall under Tsujimura's question about nonabelian Demushkin groups, as recorded in [the problem](demushkin-groups-inside-their-automorphism-groups.md). Rank at least $3$ with $q \ne 0$, which includes $G_K(p)$ for $p$-adic $K$ containing $\zeta_p$, is not treated here.
- *Why rank $2$ differs.* For pro-$p$ surface groups of rank at least $4$ and odd $p$, a closed normal copy $N$ of $D$ in $\mathrm{Aut}(D)$ that lies in $D$ is all of $D$, by the index formula $n(N) - 2 = (D : N)(n(D) - 2)$ ([pro-$p$ surface groups inside their automorphism groups](pro-p-surface-groups-inside-their-automorphism-groups.md), Step 7). In rank $2$ this formula says nothing, and indeed $D'_U \subsetneq N_0$ for odd $p$.
- *Where $p = 2$ differs.* The outer automorphism $\varepsilon$ exists because $(1 - c)/2 \in \mathbb{Z}_2$ for every $c \in \mathbb{Z}_2^\times$. For odd $p$, taking $c = -1$ shows that $G_p$ has no such room.
- *Sources.* The statements and proofs are not taken from a source, and no novelty is claimed: the computation is elementary. The literature was not searched for it.
