**Setting.** Let $g \ge 2$, and let $\bar V$ be an $\mathbb{F}_2$-vector space with basis $e_1, \dots, e_g, f_1, \dots, f_g$. Let $\langle\ ,\ \rangle$ be the bilinear form with $\langle e_i, f_j\rangle = \langle f_j, e_i\rangle = \delta_{ij}$ and $\langle e_i, e_j\rangle = \langle f_i, f_j\rangle = 0$. It is nondegenerate and symmetric, and it is alternating: $\langle x, x\rangle = 0$ for every $x$. Let $\mathrm{Sp}(\bar V)$ be the group of $h \in \mathrm{GL}(\bar V)$ with $\langle hx, hy\rangle = \langle x, y\rangle$ for all $x, y$. For $u \in \bar V$ put
$$t_u(x) = x + \langle x, u\rangle u.$$
Call $A \in \mathrm{End}(\bar V)$ *self-adjoint* if $\langle Ax, y\rangle = \langle x, Ay\rangle$ for all $x, y$. If $A$ is self-adjoint and $h \in \mathrm{Sp}(\bar V)$, then so is $hAh^{-1}$, because $\langle hAh^{-1}x, y\rangle = \langle Ah^{-1}x, h^{-1}y\rangle$. Let $L$ be the span of $e_1, \dots, e_g$. Then $L^\perp = L$: the form vanishes on $L \times L$, and if $x = \sum_i (a_ie_i + b_if_i)$ is orthogonal to $L$, then $b_j = \langle x, e_j\rangle = 0$ for every $j$.

**Theorem.** This is proved here. Each $t_u$ lies in $\mathrm{Sp}(\bar V)$. Let $G \subseteq \mathrm{Sp}(\bar V)$ be a subgroup containing $t_u$ for every $u \in \bar V$.

1. $G$ acts transitively on $\bar V \setminus \{0\}$. In particular $\bar V$ is an irreducible $G$-module.
2. Let $W \subseteq \mathrm{End}(\bar V)$ be an $\mathbb{F}_2$-subspace with $hWh^{-1} = W$ for every $h \in G$, such that $G$ acts transitively on $W \setminus \{0\}$ by conjugation. Then $\dim W \le 2$.

**Use.** For $p = 2$, part 2 replaces, in Steps 3 and 4 of the proof that [pro-$p$ surface groups are characteristic in their automorphism groups](pro-p-surface-groups-inside-their-automorphism-groups.md), the argument with an automorphism lifting $-I$, which is useless when $p = 2$. There $W$ is the image, in a layer of the congruence filtration of $\mathrm{GL}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}_2)$, of a subgroup isomorphic to the surface group, $\dim W = 2g$, and $W$ is not known in advance to be isomorphic, as a module, to $\bar V$ or to a twist of $\bar V$ by an automorphism of $G$. Part 2 needs no such identification. For free pro-$2$ groups, Tsujimura makes the identification using the automorphisms of $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{F}_2)$ [@tsujimura2026freeaut, Lemma 2.2, p. 7].

**Proof that $t_u \in \mathrm{Sp}(\bar V)$, and of 1.** Since the form is symmetric and $\langle u, u\rangle = 0$,
$$\langle t_ux, t_uy\rangle = \langle x, y\rangle + \langle x, u\rangle\langle u, y\rangle + \langle y, u\rangle\langle x, u\rangle + \langle x, u\rangle\langle y, u\rangle\langle u, u\rangle = \langle x, y\rangle,$$
and $t_u(t_ux) = x + 2\langle x, u\rangle u + \langle x, u\rangle\langle u, u\rangle u = x$, so $t_u \in \mathrm{Sp}(\bar V)$. Let $v \ne v'$ be nonzero. If $\langle v, v'\rangle = 1$, then $t_{v+v'}(v) = v + \langle v, v + v'\rangle(v + v') = v'$. If $\langle v, v'\rangle = 0$, then $v, v'$ are linearly independent, so by nondegeneracy the functionals $\langle v, -\rangle$ and $\langle v', -\rangle$ are linearly independent, and some $y$ has $\langle v, y\rangle = \langle v', y\rangle = 1$. Then $t_{v+y}(v) = y$ and $t_{y+v'}(y) = v'$. A nonzero $G$-stable subspace contains a nonzero vector, hence every nonzero vector. $\square$

**Proof of 2.** Suppose that $\dim W \ge 3$, and put $O = W \setminus \{0\}$.

*Step 1: a $2$-subgroup $Q$ of $G$.* Let $1 \le k \le g - 1$, and put $a = e_k$ and $b = f_{k+1}$, so that $\langle a, b\rangle = 0$. For $x \in \bar V$, with $\alpha = \langle x, a\rangle$ and $\beta = \langle x, b\rangle$, we get $t_{a+b}x = x + (\alpha + \beta)(a + b)$, then, as $\langle t_{a+b}x, b\rangle = \beta$, $t_bt_{a+b}x = x + (\alpha + \beta)a + \alpha b$, and then, as $\langle t_bt_{a+b}x, a\rangle = \alpha$,
$$m_k(x) := t_at_bt_{a+b}(x) = x + \langle x, f_{k+1}\rangle e_k + \langle x, e_k\rangle f_{k+1}.$$
So $m_k \in G$ fixes every basis vector except $e_{k+1} \mapsto e_{k+1} + e_k$ and $f_k \mapsto f_k + f_{k+1}$. Let $Q \subseteq G$ be the subgroup generated by the $t_u$ with $u \in L$ and by $m_1, \dots, m_{g-1}$.

Let $F_j$ be the span of $e_1, \dots, e_j$ for $0 \le j \le g$, and $F_{g+i} = L + \mathrm{span}(f_g, f_{g-1}, \dots, f_{g-i+1})$ for $0 \le i \le g$, so that $\dim F_j = j$. Each generator $h$ of $Q$ satisfies $(h - 1)F_j \subseteq F_{j-1}$ for $1 \le j \le 2g$. For $t_u$ with $u \in L$: $(t_u - 1)x = \langle x, u\rangle u$ lies in $L = F_g$, and vanishes for $x \in L$ since $L$ is isotropic. For $m_k$ and $x \in F_j$ with $j \le g$: $\langle x, e_k\rangle = 0$, and $\langle x, f_{k+1}\rangle$ is the $e_{k+1}$-coordinate of $x$, which is $0$ unless $k + 1 \le j$, when $e_k \in F_{j-1}$. For $m_k$ and $x \in F_{g+i}$ with $i \ge 1$: the term $\langle x, f_{k+1}\rangle e_k$ lies in $L \subseteq F_{g+i-1}$, and $\langle x, e_k\rangle$ is the $f_k$-coordinate of $x$, which is $0$ unless $k \ge g - i + 1$, when $f_{k+1} \in F_{g+i-1}$. The elements $h \in \mathrm{GL}(\bar V)$ with $(h - 1)F_j \subseteq F_{j-1}$ for all $j$ are, in a basis adapted to the flag $(F_j)$, the upper unitriangular matrices, a group of order $2^{g(2g-1)}$. So $Q$ is a $2$-group.

*Step 2: the centralizer of $Q$.* Define $E, N \in \mathrm{End}(\bar V)$ by
$$Ex = \langle x, e_1\rangle e_1, \qquad Nx = \langle x, e_2\rangle e_1 + \langle x, e_1\rangle e_2.$$
We show that every $A \in \mathrm{End}(\bar V)$ commuting with $Q$ lies in the span of $I$, $E$ and $N$. Let $u \in L$ be nonzero. From $At_u = t_uA$ we get $\langle x, u\rangle Au = \langle Ax, u\rangle u$ for all $x$. Taking $x$ with $\langle x, u\rangle = 1$ gives $Au = a_uu$ for some $a_u \in \mathbb{F}_2$, and then $\langle Ax, u\rangle = a_u\langle x, u\rangle$ for all $x$. For linearly independent $u, u' \in L$, comparing $A(u + u') = a_{u+u'}(u + u')$ with $a_uu + a_{u'}u'$ gives $a_u = a_{u'}$; as $\dim L = g \ge 2$, there is $c \in \mathbb{F}_2$ with $Au = cu$ and $\langle Ax, u\rangle = c\langle x, u\rangle$ for all $u \in L$ and $x \in \bar V$. Put $A' = A - cI$. Then $A'L = 0$ and $\langle A'x, u\rangle = 0$ for $u \in L$, so $A'\bar V \subseteq L^\perp = L$. Write $A'f_j = \sum_i n_{ij}e_i$.

Now $A'$ commutes with each $m_k$, and $m_k(L) \subseteq L$, so both $A'm_k$ and $m_kA'$ kill $L$. On $f_j$: $A'm_kf_j = A'f_j + \delta_{jk}A'f_{k+1}$ and $m_kA'f_j = A'f_j + n_{k+1,j}e_k$. Comparing coefficients of $e_i$ gives $\delta_{jk}n_{i,k+1} = \delta_{ik}n_{k+1,j}$ for all $i, j$. That is, for $1 \le k \le g - 1$: $n_{i,k+1} = 0$ for $i \ne k$; $n_{k+1,j} = 0$ for $j \ne k$; and $n_{k,k+1} = n_{k+1,k}$. So for $2 \le r \le g$, row $r$ and column $r$ of $(n_{ij})$ vanish except possibly at the entries $n_{r,r-1}$ and $n_{r-1,r}$. If $r \ge 3$, then $n_{r,r-1}$ lies in column $r - 1 \ge 2$ but not in row $r - 2$, so it is $0$, and then so is $n_{r-1,r} = n_{r,r-1}$. What remains is $n_{11}$ and $n_{12} = n_{21}$. Since $E$ maps $f_1 \mapsto e_1$ and $N$ maps $f_1 \mapsto e_2$, $f_2 \mapsto e_1$, both killing the other basis vectors, $A' = n_{11}E + n_{12}N$.

*Step 3: consequences.* $E$ and $N$ map $\bar V$ into $L$ and kill $L$, so every product of two of them, including $E^2$ and $N^2$, is $0$. They are self-adjoint, because $\langle Ex, y\rangle = \langle x, e_1\rangle\langle y, e_1\rangle$ and $\langle Nx, y\rangle = \langle x, e_2\rangle\langle y, e_1\rangle + \langle x, e_1\rangle\langle y, e_2\rangle$ are symmetric in $x, y$. Let $B = cI + aE + bN \ne 0$ with $a, b, c \in \mathbb{F}_2$. Then $B^2 = cI$. If $c = 1$, $B$ is invertible, of rank $2g$. If $c = 0$ and $b = 0$, then $B = E$ has rank $1$. If $c = 0$ and $b = 1$, then $B$ maps $f_1 \mapsto ae_1 + e_2$ and $f_2 \mapsto e_1$ and kills the other basis vectors, so it has rank $2$.

Let $w \in O$. Its stabilizer in $G$ is its centralizer $C_G(w)$, whose index is $|O| = 2^{\dim W} - 1$, which is odd. So $C_G(w)$ contains a Sylow $2$-subgroup $S$ of $G$. By Sylow's theorem the $2$-subgroup $Q$ lies in $hSh^{-1}$ for some $h \in G$. Then $hwh^{-1} \in O$ commutes with $Q$, so it is one of the matrices $B$ above. Since $O$ is a single orbit and conjugation preserves rank, squares and, by the Setting, self-adjointness, we conclude:

- every element of $W$ is self-adjoint;
- all elements of $O$ have the same rank $r \in \{1, 2, 2g\}$, and these three values are distinct since $g \ge 2$;
- if $r = 2g$, then $w^2 = I$ for every $w \in O$.

*Step 4: $r \ne 2g$.* Suppose $r = 2g$. For distinct $u, v \in O$, also $u + v \in O$, so $I = (u + v)^2 = u^2 + uv + vu + v^2 = uv + vu$, as $u^2 = v^2 = I$. Choose linearly independent $w_1, w_2, w_3 \in W$. Then $w_2 + w_3 \in O$ differs from $w_1$, so $I = w_1(w_2 + w_3) + (w_2 + w_3)w_1 = (w_1w_2 + w_2w_1) + (w_1w_3 + w_3w_1) = I + I = 0$, which is absurd.

*Step 5: $r \ne 1$.* Suppose $r = 1$. Every $w \in O$ has the form $x \mapsto \varphi_w(x)v_w$ with $v_w \in \bar V$ and $\varphi_w \in \bar V^*$ nonzero, both determined by $w$ since the only nonzero scalar is $1$. If $w, w' \in O$ had $v_w \ne v_{w'}$ and $\varphi_w \ne \varphi_{w'}$, then, as $\varphi_w, \varphi_{w'}$ are linearly independent, there would be $x, x'$ with $\varphi_w(x) = \varphi_{w'}(x') = 1$ and $\varphi_{w'}(x) = \varphi_w(x') = 0$, and the image of $w + w' \in O$ would contain the independent vectors $v_w$ and $v_{w'}$, contradicting $r = 1$. Fix $w_0 \in O$. Then $W$ is the union of the subspaces $\{w : w\bar V \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2v_{w_0}\}$ and $\{w : w(\ker\varphi_{w_0}) = 0\}$, so it equals one of them, as a vector space is not a union of two proper subspaces. In the first case every $w \in O$ has image $\mathbb{F}_2v_{w_0}$; since $hwh^{-1}$ has image $h(w\bar V)$, this line is $G$-stable. In the second case every $w \in O$ has kernel $\ker\varphi_{w_0}$, and this hyperplane is $G$-stable. Both contradict part 1.

*Step 6: $r \ne 2$.* Suppose $r = 2$, and for $w \in O$ let $P_w = w\bar V$, a plane. As $w$ is self-adjoint, $\ker w = P_w^\perp$: $\langle wx, y\rangle = 0$ for all $y$ if and only if $\langle x, wy\rangle = 0$ for all $y$. Let $w_1 \ne w_2$ in $O$, and suppose $P_{w_1} \cap P_{w_2} = 0$. Then $\dim(P_{w_1}^\perp \cap P_{w_2}^\perp) = \dim (P_{w_1} + P_{w_2})^\perp = 2g - 4$, so $w_1$ maps $P_{w_2}^\perp$, of dimension $2g - 2$, onto a space of dimension $2$, which is $P_{w_1}$. Thus $(w_1 + w_2)(P_{w_2}^\perp) = P_{w_1}$, and likewise $(w_1 + w_2)(P_{w_1}^\perp) = P_{w_2}$, so $w_1 + w_2 \in O$ has rank $4$, a contradiction. So any two of the planes $P_w$ meet nontrivially.

Since $P_{hwh^{-1}} = hP_w$, the family $\{P_w : w \in O\}$ is permuted by $G$, and so the sum and the intersection of all the $P_w$ are $G$-stable subspaces. If all $P_w$ are equal, their sum is a $G$-stable plane, contradicting part 1 as $2 < 2g$. Otherwise take $P_{w_1} \ne P_{w_2}$. They meet in a line $\mathbb{F}_2 a$, and span a $3$-dimensional space $T$. Every other $P_w$ contains $a$ or lies in $T$: if $a \notin P_w$, then $P_w$ meets $P_{w_1}$ and $P_{w_2}$ in lines $\mathbb{F}_2 b$ and $\mathbb{F}_2 b'$ with $b, b' \ne a$, so $b \ne b'$, as $P_{w_1} \cap P_{w_2} = \mathbb{F}_2 a$, and $P_w = \mathrm{span}(b, b') \subseteq T$. If some $P_{w_3} \not\ni a$ and some $P_{w_4} \not\subseteq T$, then $a \in P_{w_4}$, so $P_{w_4} \cap T = \mathbb{F}_2 a$, and $0 \ne P_{w_3} \cap P_{w_4} \subseteq T \cap P_{w_4}$ forces $a \in P_{w_3}$, a contradiction. So either every $P_w$ contains $a$, and the intersection of all the $P_w$ is the $G$-stable line $\mathbb{F}_2 a$; or every $P_w$ lies in $T$, and their sum is the $G$-stable space $T$ of dimension $3 < 2g$. Both contradict part 1.

Steps 3–6 exclude every value of $r$, so $\dim W \le 2$. $\square$

**Remarks.**

- *Where $g \ge 2$ is used.* In Step 2, through $\dim L \ge 2$ and the elements $e_2$ and $m_1$; in Step 3, to separate the ranks $2$ and $2g$; and in Step 6, through $3 < 2g$.
- *Sources.* The statement and proof are not taken from a source, and no novelty is claimed for this elementary fact.
