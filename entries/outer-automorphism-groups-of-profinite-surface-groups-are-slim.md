**Setting.** Let $g \ge 2$, and let $\Pi = \widehat{\Gamma}_{g,0}$ be the profinite group on $x_1, y_1, \dots, x_g, y_g$ with the single relation $[x_1, y_1] \cdots [x_g, y_g] = 1$. It is the étale fundamental group of every proper smooth connected curve of genus $g$ over an algebraically closed field of characteristic $0$, and the profinite completion of the fundamental group of a closed orientable surface of genus $g$ ([geometric fundamental groups of curves](geometric-fundamental-groups-of-curves.md), parts 2 and 4).

A profinite group $G$ is *slim* if the centralizer $Z_G(H)$ of every open subgroup $H$ is trivial. A finite normal closed subgroup of a slim profinite group is trivial [@mochizuki2012, §0, p. 8]. Minamide, Sawada and Tsujimura prove that every pro-$\mathcal{C}$ surface group, for a nontrivial full formation $\mathcal{C}$, is strongly sn-internally indecomposable and sn-elastic [@minamidesawadatsujimura2022, Theorem 3.13, p. 21]. Taking $\mathcal{C}$ to be the class of all finite groups, $\Pi$ is such a group, since it is the fundamental group of a hyperbolic curve over an algebraically closed field of characteristic $0$ [@minamidesawadatsujimura2022, Definition 3.12, p. 21]. By their definitions, this says in particular that for every open subgroup $H \subseteq \Pi$ and every nontrivial closed normal subgroup $S$ of $H$, the centralizer $Z_H(S)$ is trivial [@minamidesawadatsujimura2022, Definition 1.8, p. 9, and Proposition 1.7, p. 8]. Given an open subgroup $H \subseteq \Pi$, apply this to the open subgroup $\Pi$ and to an open normal subgroup $S \subseteq H$ of $\Pi$: then $Z_\Pi(H) \subseteq Z_\Pi(S) = 1$. So $\Pi$ is slim, and in particular centre-free. As $\Pi$ is centre-free and topologically finitely generated, $1 \to \Pi \to \mathrm{Aut}(\Pi) \to \mathrm{Out}(\Pi) \to 1$ is an exact sequence of profinite groups, for the topology defined by the characteristic open subgroups of $\Pi$ [@mochizuki2012, §0, p. 9].

Let $k \subset \bar k$ be a number field and an algebraic closure, and let $C$ be a smooth proper geometrically connected curve of genus $g$ over $k$, a hyperbolic curve of type $(g, 0)$. Omit base points, and write $\Pi_C = \pi_1(C)$ and $\Delta = \pi_1(C_{\bar k})$, so that $1 \to \Delta \to \Pi_C \to G_k \to 1$ is exact and $\Delta \cong \Pi$ ([geometric fundamental groups of curves](geometric-fundamental-groups-of-curves.md), parts 1 and 4). Conjugation in $\Pi_C$ gives the outer Galois representation $\rho : G_k \to \mathrm{Out}(\Delta)$ ([hyperbolic curve](hyperbolic-curve.md)). An automorphism $f$ of $C_L$, for $L/k$ finite, induces an automorphism of $\pi_1(C_L)$ over $G_L$, well defined up to conjugation by $\Delta$; its restriction to $\Delta$ defines $f_* \in \mathrm{Out}(\Delta)$.

**Theorem.** This is proved here.

1. *Centralizers of Galois images.* For every finite extension $L \subset \bar k$ of $k$, the map $\mathrm{Aut}_L(C_L) \to \mathrm{Out}(\Delta)$, $f \mapsto f_*$, is an injective homomorphism, and its image is the centralizer of $\rho(G_L)$ in $\mathrm{Out}(\Delta)$.
2. *Finite normal subgroups.* Fix an isomorphism $\Delta \cong \Pi$. Every finite normal closed subgroup of $\mathrm{Out}(\Pi)$ lies in the image of $\mathrm{Aut}_L(C_L)$ for some finite $L/k$. In particular it is isomorphic to a subgroup of $\mathrm{Aut}(C_{\bar k})$.
3. *Genus at least $3$.* If $g \ge 3$, then $\mathrm{Out}(\Pi)$ is slim. In particular $\mathrm{Out}(\Pi)$ has trivial centre and no nontrivial finite normal closed subgroup.
4. *Genus $2$.* If $g = 2$, then every finite normal closed subgroup $F \ne 1$ of $\mathrm{Out}(\Pi)$ has order $2$ and is central. If $C$ is a curve of genus $2$ over a number field with $\mathrm{Aut}(C_{\bar k}) = \{1, \iota\}$, then for every isomorphism $\Delta \cong \Pi$, $F$ is generated by the image of $\iota_*$.

No novelty is claimed. The argument is a direct application of the Grothendieck conjecture, and statements of this kind may be in the literature, but no source stating them for surface groups was opened. Whether a nontrivial finite normal subgroup of $\mathrm{Out}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{2,0})$ exists, that is, whether the class of $\iota_*$ in part 4 is central, is not decided here.

**Proof of part 1.** Write $P = \pi_1(C_L)$.

*Step 1: $P$ inside $\Pi_C$.* The fundamental exact sequence for $C_L$ over $L$ reads $1 \to \Delta \to P \to G_L \to 1$, because $C_L \otimes_L \bar k = C_{\bar k}$. The morphism $C_L \to C$ induces a homomorphism $P \to \Pi_C$ compatible with the inclusion $G_L \subseteq G_k$ and with the identity of $\Delta$. A diagram chase shows that it is injective with image the inverse image of $G_L$. So the outer representation of $C_L$ is $\rho|_{G_L}$.

*Step 2: $P$ as a fibre product.* Let $c : P \to \mathrm{Aut}(\Delta)$ be conjugation, $c(x)(\delta) = x\delta x^{-1}$, and $\mathrm{pr} : P \to G_L$ the projection. Then $c$ is continuous: for a characteristic open $V \subseteq \Delta$, the elements acting trivially on the finite group $\Delta/V$ form an open subgroup of $P$, as $\Delta/V$ is generated by the images of finitely many $\delta_i$ and each condition $x\delta_ix^{-1} \in \delta_iV$ is open. Put
$$E = \{(\alpha, \sigma) \in \mathrm{Aut}(\Delta) \times G_L : [\alpha] = \rho(\sigma)\},$$
a closed subgroup. The map $x \mapsto (c(x), \mathrm{pr}(x))$ is a continuous homomorphism $P \to E$. It is injective: if $c(x) = 1$ and $\mathrm{pr}(x) = 1$, then $x \in \Delta$ is central in $\Delta$, so $x = 1$. It is surjective: given $(\alpha, \sigma) \in E$, pick $y$ with $\mathrm{pr}(y) = \sigma$; then $[c(y)] = [\alpha]$, so $\alpha = c(y) \circ c(\delta) = c(y\delta)$ for some $\delta \in \Delta$, and $\mathrm{pr}(y\delta) = \sigma$. A continuous bijection of compact Hausdorff groups is an isomorphism, so $P \cong E$.

*Step 3: the image commutes with $\rho(G_L)$.* Let $f \in \mathrm{Aut}_L(C_L)$, and let $\Psi$ be an automorphism of $P$ over $G_L$ inducing it. For $x \in P$ with image $\sigma$, $\Psi(x)$ also maps to $\sigma$, so $\Psi(x) = x\delta_x$ with $\delta_x \in \Delta$. Applying $\Psi$ to $x\delta x^{-1}$ gives $\Psi|_\Delta \circ c(x) = c(x) \circ c(\delta_x) \circ \Psi|_\Delta$ on $\Delta$. In $\mathrm{Out}(\Delta)$ this says $f_*\rho(\sigma) = \rho(\sigma)f_*$. As $f \mapsto f_*$ is induced by the functoriality of $\pi_1$, it is a homomorphism.

*Step 4: injectivity.* Suppose $f_* = 1$, so that $\Psi|_\Delta = c(\delta_0)|_\Delta$ for some $\delta_0 \in \Delta$. Then $\Psi' = c(\delta_0)^{-1} \circ \Psi$ is an automorphism of $P$ over $G_L$ that is the identity on $\Delta$. For $x \in P$ and $\delta \in \Delta$, $\Psi'(x)\delta\Psi'(x)^{-1} = \Psi'(x\delta x^{-1}) = x\delta x^{-1}$, so $x^{-1}\Psi'(x)$ centralizes $\Delta$. It lies in $\Delta$, since $\Psi'$ is over $G_L$, so it is $1$, as $\Delta$ is centre-free. Thus $\Psi \equiv \mathrm{id}$ modulo conjugation by $\Delta$. By the Grothendieck conjecture over $L$, which is finitely generated over $\mathbb{Q}$, the $L$-automorphisms of $C_L$ correspond bijectively to the automorphisms of $P$ over $G_L$ modulo conjugation by $\Delta$ ([Mochizuki's Hom theorem](mochizuki-hom-theorem-for-hyperbolic-curves.md)). Both $f$ and $\mathrm{id}_{C_L}$ correspond to the class of the identity, so $f = \mathrm{id}$.

*Step 5: the centralizer lies in the image.* Let $\varphi \in \mathrm{Out}(\Delta)$ commute with $\rho(\sigma)$ for every $\sigma \in G_L$, and choose a lift $\tilde\varphi \in \mathrm{Aut}(\Delta)$. Define $\Phi : E \to E$ by $\Phi(\alpha, \sigma) = (\tilde\varphi\alpha\tilde\varphi^{-1}, \sigma)$. It lands in $E$, because $[\tilde\varphi\alpha\tilde\varphi^{-1}] = \varphi\rho(\sigma)\varphi^{-1} = \rho(\sigma)$. It is a continuous automorphism, with inverse given by $\tilde\varphi^{-1}$, and it commutes with the projection to $G_L$. Transport it to an automorphism $\Psi$ of $P$ over $G_L$ by Step 2. For $\delta \in \Delta$, $(c(\delta), 1)$ goes to $(\tilde\varphi c(\delta)\tilde\varphi^{-1}, 1) = (c(\tilde\varphi(\delta)), 1)$, so $\Psi|_\Delta = \tilde\varphi$. By the Grothendieck conjecture, $\Psi$ is induced, modulo conjugation by $\Delta$, by some $f \in \mathrm{Aut}_L(C_L)$, and then $f_* = [\tilde\varphi] = \varphi$. $\square$

**Proof of part 2.** First, $\rho$ is continuous. The images in $\mathrm{Out}(\Delta)$ of the kernels $K_V$ of $\mathrm{Aut}(\Delta) \to \mathrm{Aut}(\Delta/V)$, for $V \subseteq \Delta$ characteristic and open, form a basis of open neighbourhoods of $1$. The elements of $\Pi_C$ acting trivially on $\Delta/V$ form an open subgroup, as in Step 2, and its image in $G_k$ is open, because a surjective homomorphism of profinite groups is open. That image lies in $\rho^{-1}$ of the image of $K_V$. So for every open subgroup $U$ of $\mathrm{Out}(\Delta)$, the subgroup $\rho^{-1}(U)$ contains an open subgroup of $G_k$, and is therefore open.

Let $F$ be a finite normal closed subgroup of $\mathrm{Out}(\Pi) \cong \mathrm{Out}(\Delta)$. Conjugation gives a homomorphism $\mathrm{Out}(\Delta) \to \mathrm{Sym}(F)$, and its kernel $Z(F)$, the centralizer of $F$, is an intersection of finitely many closed subgroups $\{y : yuy^{-1} = u\}$, $u \in F$. So it is closed of finite index, hence open. Then $\rho^{-1}(Z(F))$ is an open subgroup of $G_k$, so it contains $G_L$ for some finite $L/k$. Now $F$ centralizes $\rho(G_L)$, so by part 1 it lies in the image of $\mathrm{Aut}_L(C_L)$, which is isomorphic to $\mathrm{Aut}_L(C_L)$. Finally $\mathrm{Aut}_L(C_L)$ embeds in $\mathrm{Aut}(C_{\bar k})$ by base change, which is injective because $C_{\bar k} \to C_L$ is faithfully flat. $\square$

**Proof of part 3.** Let $g \ge 3$. Poonen proves that for every field $k$ and every $g \ge 3$ there is a smooth projective geometrically integral curve $C$ of genus $g$ over $k$ whose automorphism group over $\bar k$ is trivial [@poonen2000curves, Theorem 1, p. 1]. Take $k = \mathbb{Q}$ and fix $\Delta \cong \Pi$. By part 1, $\rho(G_L)$ has trivial centralizer in $\mathrm{Out}(\Delta)$ for every finite $L/\mathbb{Q}$. Let $U \subseteq \mathrm{Out}(\Delta)$ be an open subgroup. Then $\rho^{-1}(U)$ is open in $G_{\mathbb{Q}}$, by the continuity shown in the proof of part 2, so it contains $G_L$ for some finite $L$, and $Z(U) \subseteq Z(\rho(G_L)) = 1$. So $\mathrm{Out}(\Pi)$ is slim. Its centre is $Z(\mathrm{Out}(\Pi))$, the centralizer of the open subgroup $\mathrm{Out}(\Pi)$, so it is trivial, and finite normal closed subgroups are trivial by the general fact recalled in the setting. $\square$

**Proof of part 4.** Let $g = 2$. Poonen proves that for every field $k$ and every $g \ge 2$ there is a hyperelliptic curve $C$ of genus $g$ over $k$ whose automorphism group over $\bar k$ is $\{1, \iota\}$, with $\iota$ the hyperelliptic involution [@poonen2000hyperelliptic, Theorem 1, p. 1]. Let $C$ be any genus-$2$ curve over a number field with $\mathrm{Aut}(C_{\bar k}) = \{1, \iota\}$, such as Poonen's curve over $\mathbb{Q}$, and fix $\Delta \cong \Pi$. Let $F \ne 1$ be a finite normal closed subgroup of $\mathrm{Out}(\Pi)$. By part 2, $F$ lies in the image of $\mathrm{Aut}_L(C_L)$ for some $L$, a group of order at most $2$, since $\mathrm{Aut}_L(C_L)$ embeds in $\{1, \iota\}$. As $F \ne 1$, $\mathrm{Aut}_L(C_L) = \{1, \iota\}$ and $F$ is generated by $\iota_*$, which is nontrivial by part 1. A normal subgroup of order $2$ is central, because conjugation must fix its only nontrivial element. $\square$
