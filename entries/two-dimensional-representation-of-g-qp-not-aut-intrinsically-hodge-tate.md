**Setting.** Let $p$ be odd, $G = G_{\mathbb{Q}_p}$, $K \subset \bar{\mathbb{Q}}_p$ the unramified quadratic extension of $\mathbb{Q}_p$, and $\iota$ its nontrivial automorphism. Let $\chi = \chi_{p,\mathrm{id}} : G_K^{\mathrm{ab}} \to \mathcal{O}_K^\times$ be the inverse of $\mathrm{rec}_K : (K^\times)^\wedge \to G_K^{\mathrm{ab}}$ followed by the projection to $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$ that kills $p$, and $\rho_K : G_K \to \mathcal{O}_K^\times \subset \mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbb{Q}_p}(K_+)$ the resulting Lubin–Tate representation [@hoshi2024, Definition 1.7, p. 5]. A representation $\rho$ of $G$ on a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{Q}_p$-vector space is *Aut-intrinsically Hodge–Tate* if $\rho \circ \theta$ is Hodge–Tate for every automorphism $\theta$ of $G$ [@hoshi2024, Definition 1.3, p. 3]; see [Aut-intrinsic Hodge–Tate representations](aut-intrinsic-hodge-tate-representations.md). For $\theta \in \mathrm{Aut}(G)$, let $b(\theta) \in \mathbb{Z}_p^\times$ be as in [automorphisms of $G_{\mathbb{Q}_p}$ on norm-one units](automorphisms-of-the-galois-group-of-qp-scale-norm-one-units.md): $\theta$ preserves $G_K$, and through $\mathrm{rec}_K$ it fixes $U^1_{\mathbb{Q}_p}$ and acts on the norm-one principal units $N^1$ of $K$ by $n \mapsto n^{b(\theta)}$.

**Theorem.** This is proved here.

1. *An extension.* Fix $s \in G \setminus G_K$ and $c \in \mathcal{O}_K^\times$ with $c\,\iota(c) = \chi(s^2)$. For $h \in G_K$ and $x \in K$ put
$$W(h)x = \chi(h)\,x,\qquad W(sh)x = c\,\iota\big(\chi(h)\,x\big).$$
Then $W$ is a continuous representation of $G$ on the $2$-dimensional $\mathbb{Q}_p$-vector space $K$, with $W|_{G_K} = \rho_K$. It is irreducible, even restricted to $G_K$, and it is Hodge–Tate.
2. *The criterion.* For $\theta \in \mathrm{Aut}(G)$, $W \circ \theta$ is Hodge–Tate if and only if $b(\theta)$ is an odd rational integer. The same holds for every representation of $G$ whose restriction to $G_K$ is isomorphic to $\rho_K$.
3. *Failure for $p \ge 5$.* If $p \ge 5$, then $W$ is not Aut-intrinsically Hodge–Tate. Indeed some $\theta$ has $b(\theta) \not\equiv \pm 1 \pmod p$, and then $W \circ \theta$ or $W \circ \theta^{-1}$ is not Hodge–Tate.

So for $p \ge 5$, $2$ is the smallest dimension of a Hodge–Tate representation of $G_{\mathbb{Q}_p}$ that is not Aut-intrinsically Hodge–Tate: in dimension $1$, and for reducible representations of dimension $2$, Hodge–Tate implies Aut-intrinsically Hodge–Tate over every $p$-adic field [@hoshi2024, Theorems 2.7 and 2.10, pp. 8–9].

**Context.** Hoshi asked whether a $p$-adic field $k$ has a Hodge–Tate representation of dimension $2$ that is not Aut-intrinsically Hodge–Tate [@hoshi2024, Introduction, p. 2]. For each odd $p$ he found one over some finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}_p$ [@hoshi2024, Theorem B and Corollary 3.4, pp. 2 and 11]. His examples are Lubin–Tate representations $\rho_k$ of absolutely abelian $k$ of even degree, and his automorphism of $G_k$, built from a quadratic subfield, moves $\mathcal{O}^\times_{\mathbb{Q}_p} \subset \mathcal{O}_k^\times$ [@hoshi2024, Lemma 3.1 (v), Proposition 3.2 and Theorem 3.3, pp. 9–11]. That cannot happen for an automorphism of $G_K$ extended from $G_{\mathbb{Q}_p}$, which is why $k = \mathbb{Q}_p$ needs different automorphisms, those of part 2 of the companion entry: they fix $U^1_{\mathbb{Q}_p}$ and change the exponent on $N^1$. For $p = 3$ the question for $G_{\mathbb{Q}_3}$ remains open here, because the available automorphisms only control $b$ modulo $3$, where $\pm 1$ are the only units. The set of $\theta$ with $W \circ \theta$ Hodge–Tate is $b^{-1}(1 + 2\mathbb{Z})$, a union of cosets of $\ker b$; inner automorphisms have $b = \pm 1$ and lie in it.

**Proof of part 1.**

- *Compatibility.* For $h \in G_K$ and $t \notin G_K$, $\chi(tht^{-1}) = \iota(\chi(h))$. Indeed reciprocity commutes with conjugation, $\mathrm{rec}_K(\iota x) = t\,\mathrm{rec}_K(x)\,t^{-1}$ [@neukirchschmidtwingberg2008, Chapter III, §2, p. 170; (7.1.5), p. 375], and $\iota(p) = p$, so the projection killing $p$ commutes with $\iota$. In particular $\iota(\chi(s^2)) = \chi(s s^2 s^{-1}) = \chi(s^2)$, so $\chi(s^2) \in \mathbb{Z}_p^\times$. The norm $\mathcal{O}_K^\times \to \mathbb{Z}_p^\times$ is surjective because $K/\mathbb{Q}_p$ is unramified [@milnecft, Chapter III, Proposition 1.2, p. 98], so $c$ exists.
- *Homomorphism.* Write $m_a$ for multiplication by $a \in K^\times$, so $\iota m_a = m_{\iota(a)}\iota$ and $\iota^2 = 1$. For $h, h' \in G_K$: $W(h)W(h') = W(hh')$ is clear; $W(sh)W(h') = m_c\iota m_{\chi(h)\chi(h')} = W(shh')$; $W(h)W(sh') = m_{\chi(h)}m_c\iota m_{\chi(h')} = m_c\iota m_{\iota\chi(h)\chi(h')} = W(s\,(s^{-1}hs)\,h')$, using $\chi(s^{-1}hs) = \iota\chi(h)$; and
$$W(sh)W(sh') = m_c\iota m_{\chi(h)}m_c\iota m_{\chi(h')} = m_{c\,\iota(c)\,\iota\chi(h)\,\chi(h')} = m_{\chi(s^2)\chi(s^{-1}hs)\chi(h')} = W(s^2\,(s^{-1}hs)\,h') = W(shsh').$$
Continuity holds because $\chi$ is continuous and $G_K$ is open.
- *Irreducibility.* $\chi$ maps $G_K$ onto $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$, which spans $K$ over $\mathbb{Q}_p$. So a $G_K$-stable $\mathbb{Q}_p$-subspace is a $K$-subspace, hence $0$ or $K$.
- *Hodge–Tate.* $\rho_K$ is Hodge–Tate ([non-Galois Lubin–Tate representations](non-galois-lubin-tate-representations-are-not-aut-intrinsically-hodge-tate.md), part 1; for Galois $K$ it is crystalline [@kondo2026, Remark 2.4]). A representation of $G$ is Hodge–Tate if and only if its restriction to $G_K$ is: restriction keeps Hodge–Tate-ness because $B_{\mathrm{HT}}^{G_K} = K$, and Galois descent along $K/\mathbb{Q}_p$ gives the converse, as in the proof of part 1 of that entry. $\square$

**Proof of part 2.** By the last fact, $W \circ \theta$ is Hodge–Tate exactly when its restriction $\rho_K \circ \theta_K$ is, where $\theta_K = \theta|_{G_K}$. This only involves $W|_{G_K}$, which gives the last sentence of part 2. The representation $\rho_K \circ \theta_K$ is $G_K \to G_K^{\mathrm{ab}} \xrightarrow{\phi} \mathcal{O}_K^\times \subset \mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbb{Q}_p}(K_+)$ with $\phi = \chi \circ \theta_K^{\mathrm{ab}}$. Hoshi's lemma applies with $k = E = K$, which is Galois over $\mathbb{Q}_p$, and uniformizer $p$. It says that this is Hodge–Tate if and only if there are integers $i_1, i_\iota$ and an open subgroup $J$ of the inertia group of $G_K$ on which $\phi$ agrees with $\chi_{p,\mathrm{id}}^{i_1}\chi_{p,\iota}^{i_\iota}$, where $\chi_{p,\iota} = \iota \circ \chi$ [@hoshi2024, Definition 1.7 and Lemma 1.8, p. 5].

The image of inertia in $G_K^{\mathrm{ab}}$ is $\mathrm{rec}_K(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)$ ([local class field theory](local-class-field-theory.md)), so the images of the open subgroups $J$ are the $\mathrm{rec}_K(U')$ with $U' \subseteq \mathcal{O}_K^\times$ open. For $u \in \mathcal{O}_K^\times$, $\phi(\mathrm{rec}_K(u)) = \chi(\mathrm{rec}_K(\theta_K^\times(u))) = \theta_K^\times(u)$, in the notation of the companion entry, while $\chi_{p,\mathrm{id}}(\mathrm{rec}_K(u)) = u$ and $\chi_{p,\iota}(\mathrm{rec}_K(u)) = \iota(u)$. So $W \circ \theta$ is Hodge–Tate if and only if
$$\theta_K^\times(u) = u^{i_1}\,\iota(u)^{i_\iota}\quad\text{for all } u \text{ in some open } U' \subseteq \mathcal{O}_K^\times. \tag{$*$}$$
By the companion entry, $U^1_K = U^1_{\mathbb{Q}_p} \times N^1$ with both factors isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_p$, $\theta_K^\times$ is the identity on $U^1_{\mathbb{Q}_p}$, and it is $n \mapsto n^{b(\theta)}$ on $N^1$. On $u = xn$ with $x \in U^1_{\mathbb{Q}_p}$, $n \in N^1$, the right side of $(*)$ is $x^{i_1 + i_\iota}n^{i_1 - i_\iota}$.

- If $b(\theta) = 2i - 1$ with $i \in \mathbb{Z}$, then $i_1 = i$, $i_\iota = 1 - i$ and $U' = U^1_K$ satisfy $(*)$.
- Conversely, suppose $(*)$ holds. $U' \cap U^1_{\mathbb{Q}_p}$ and $U' \cap N^1$ are open, hence nontrivial, subgroups of torsion-free groups. Comparing exponents gives $i_1 + i_\iota = 1$ and $b(\theta) = i_1 - i_\iota = 2i_1 - 1$. $\square$

**Proof of part 3.** Let $p \ge 5$ and choose $c \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times \setminus \{\pm 1\}$. The companion entry, part 2, gives $\theta$ with $b(\theta) \equiv c \pmod p$, so $b(\theta) \ne \pm 1$. If $b(\theta)$ is an odd integer, then $b(\theta^{-1}) = b(\theta)^{-1}$ is not an integer, since the only integers whose inverses are integers are $\pm 1$. By part 2, $W \circ \theta$ or $W \circ \theta^{-1}$ is not Hodge–Tate. $\square$
