**Statement.** Let $S$ be a finite set of primes and $\mathcal{X} = \mathbb{P}^1_{\mathbb{Z}[1/S]} \setminus \{0, 1, \infty\}$. Then $\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{Z}[1/S])$ is finite. More precisely, Kim's proof chooses an integer $n$ depending only on $S$, and then, for every prime $p \notin S$ with $(p-1)/2 \ge n + 1$, produces a nonzero Coleman function on $\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{Z}_p)$ that vanishes on $\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{Z}[1/S])$ [@kim2005, §3, proof of Siegel's theorem, pp. 17–18].

Kim calls this a "$\pi_1$ proof", in which functions on the fundamental group give Diophantine finiteness, and a "non-abelian lift" of Chabauty's method [@kim2005, pp. 1–2]. The fundamental group is the unipotent motivic one of Deligne, used only through its étale, de Rham and crystalline realizations and the comparisons between them [@kim2005, p. 1].

**What it says, checked here.** Since $\mathcal{X} = \mathrm{Spec}\, \mathbb{Z}[1/S][z, z^{-1}, (1 - z)^{-1}]$, a point of $\mathcal{X}$ with values in a $\mathbb{Z}[1/S]$-algebra $R$ is an element $z \in R$ such that $z$ and $1 - z$ are both units. So the theorem is the finiteness of the solutions of the $S$-unit equation $u + v = 1$ in $\mathbb{Z}[1/S]^\times$. For example:

- $S = \emptyset$. Here $z, 1 - z \in \{\pm 1\}$ is impossible, so $\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{Z}) = \emptyset$.
- $S = \{2\}$. The solutions are $z = 2, -1, 1/2$. If $z = \pm 2^{-a}$ with $a \ge 1$, then $1 - z$ has the same $2$-adic valuation $-a$, so $1 - z = \pm 2^{-a}$, and $\pm 1 \pm 1 = 2^a$ forces $a = 1$ and $z = 1/2$. The case where $1 - z$ has negative valuation is symmetric, giving $z = 1/2$ again. Otherwise $z$ and $1 - z$ are $\pm$ powers of $2$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ with odd sum $1$, so one of them is $\pm 1$; this gives $z = -1$ or $z = 2$.

For $p \notin S$, the set $\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{Z}_p)$ consists of the $z \in \mathbb{Z}_p$ with $z \not\equiv 0, 1 \pmod p$. It is compact, and it is the set Kim writes as $X(\mathbb{Q}_p) \cap\, ]Y[$, the points of the generic fibre that reduce into the special fibre $Y$.

**The objects.** Fix $p \notin S$ and put $T = S \cup \{p\}$. Let $\Gamma_T$ be the Galois group of the maximal extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ unramified outside $T$, and $G = G_{\mathbb{Q}_p}$ a decomposition group at $p$. Choose an $S$-integral point $x$ as base point; if there is none, there is nothing to prove [@kim2005, p. 17].

- $\pi_{1,\text{ét}}$ is the $\mathbb{Q}_p$-unipotent completion of the étale fundamental group of $X \otimes \bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ at $x$, with its action of $\Gamma_T$, and $[\pi_{1,\text{ét}}]_n$ is its quotient along the descending central series, indexed so that $n = 1$ is the abelianization [@kim2005, pp. 14, 17].
- $\pi_{1,DR}$ is the de Rham fundamental group of $X \otimes \mathbb{Q}_p$: Deligne's Tannakian fundamental group of unipotent vector bundles with connection. It carries a Hodge filtration, and a Frobenius from its comparison with the crystalline fundamental group of the special fibre [@kim2005, pp. 11, 14–15]. Its coordinate ring is spanned by functions $\alpha_w$ indexed by the words $w$ in two letters $A, B$ [@kim2005, p. 2].
- The *unipotent Albanese map* $UAlb : \mathcal{X}(\mathbb{Z}_p) \to \pi_{1,DR}(\mathbb{Q}_p)$ measures, at each point $y$, the difference between the Frobenius-invariant path from $x$ to $y$ and the path given by the Hodge filtration [@kim2005, pp. 2, 12, 16]. Kim calls these $p$-adic analogues of Hain's higher Albanese maps, in which periods are avoided by using the Frobenius-invariant path [@kim2005, p. 12]. With a tangential base point at $0$, $UAlb(z)$ is the value $G(z) = \sum_w g_w(z)\, w$ of the solution of the $p$-adic Knizhnik–Zamolodchikov equation for the connection $A\, dz/z + B\, dz/(z - 1)$ that is asymptotic to $\exp(A \log z)$ at $0$. Its coefficients $g_w$ are Coleman functions [@kim2005, Proposition 4, p. 12], that is, $p$-adic iterated integrals [@bdckw2018, §1.7, p. 4]. For instance, in the coordinates that Balakrishnan, Dan-Cohen, Kim and Wewers use on the level-$2$ target, which is $3$-dimensional, the local map is $z \mapsto (\log z, \log(1 - z), -\mathrm{Li}_2(z))$ with $\mathrm{Li}_2$ the $p$-adic dilogarithm [@bdckw2018, §6.2, p. 44].
- *Selmer variety.* An $S$-integral point $y$ gives the torsor of étale paths from $x$ to $y$, and so a class in $H^1(\Gamma_T, [\pi_{1,\text{ét}}]_n)$. If $(p-1)/2 \ge n + 1$, this class lies in $H^1_f(\Gamma_T, [\pi_{1,\text{ét}}]_n)$, the classes whose restriction to $G$ is de Rham, by Vologodsky's comparison theorem [@kim2005, p. 17; Proposition 5, p. 15]. Kim makes these nonabelian cohomology sets into affine varieties over $\mathbb{Q}_p$ [@kim2005, Proposition 2, p. 7, and pp. 10, 15].

**Proof outline.**

1. *Linear independence.* The $g_w$ are linearly independent over the base field [@kim2005, Theorem 1, p. 13], and so are the corresponding functions for any other base point [@kim2005, p. 14]. Hence no nonzero function on $\pi_{1,DR}$ pulls back under $UAlb$ to the zero function on $\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{Z}_p)$ [@kim2005, p. 2].
2. *Global points land in an algebraic image.* By compatibility of the fundamental groups with base change and the comparison theorem, the level-$n$ map $UAlb_n$ sends $\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{Z}[1/S])$ into the image of the algebraic map $H^1_f(\Gamma_T, [\pi_{1,\text{ét}}]_n) \to H^1_f(G, [\pi_{1,\text{ét}}]_n) \to [\pi_{1,DR}]_n$ [@kim2005, pp. 2, 17].
3. *Dimensions.* The graded pieces of the central series are $\mathbb{Q}_p(m)^{r_m}$, on both the étale and the de Rham side, with ranks $r_m$ that grow to infinity and do not depend on $p$. So $\dim [\pi_{1,DR}]_n = r_1 + \cdots + r_n$ [@kim2005, p. 18]. The Selmer variety is built in layers from the $H^1_f(\Gamma_T, \mathbb{Q}_p(m)^{r_m})$ [@kim2005, p. 17]. The layer $m = 1$ contributes $R = 2\, \mathrm{rank}\, \mathbb{Z}[1/S]^\times$, which is $2|S|$ because the units of $\mathbb{Z}[1/S]$ are $\pm \prod_{\ell \in S} \ell^{a_\ell}$. For $m \ge 2$, Kim uses Soulé's theorem: $H^1(\Gamma_T, \mathbb{Q}_p(m))$ vanishes for even $m$ and is one-dimensional for odd $m$ [@kim2005, p. 18]. Hence
$$\dim H^1_f(\Gamma_T, [\pi_{1,\text{ét}}]_n) \le 2|S| + \sum_{3 \le m \le n,\ m \text{ odd}} r_m.$$
4. *Conclusion.* For $n$ large the right-hand side is less than $\dim [\pi_{1,DR}]_n$. Choose such an $n$, and then $p \notin S$ with $(p-1)/2 \ge n + 1$. The image in step 2 then lies in a proper subvariety, so some nonzero function on $[\pi_{1,DR}]_n$ vanishes on $UAlb_n(\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{Z}[1/S]))$. Its pullback is a linear combination of the $g_w$, nonzero by step 1, so it is a nonzero Coleman function vanishing on $\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{Z}[1/S])$. The identity principle for Coleman functions and the compactness of $\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{Z}_p)$ then give finiteness [@kim2005, pp. 2, 18].

*Reading notes.* In step 3, Kim's displayed bound $R + r_3 + r_5 + \cdots$ ends with the index $2\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$, and his statement of the odd case reads "$H^1(G_T, \mathbb{Q}_p(2n+1))$ has dimension 1 for $n \ge 3$". The bound itself uses every odd $m \ge 3$ up to $n$, which is what Soulé's theorem gives, so both look like misprints. In step 2, his text says that $UAlb_n(X(\mathbb{Q}_p) \cap\, ]Y[)$ lies in the image, but the diagrams just before give this for the $S$-integral points, which is what the argument uses [@kim2005, pp. 17–18].

**How large $n$ must be, checked here.** By step 3, the gap is
$$\dim [\pi_{1,DR}]_n - \Bigl(2|S| + \sum_{3 \le m \le n,\ m \text{ odd}} r_m\Bigr) = r_1 - 2|S| + \sum_{2 \le m \le n,\ m \text{ even}} r_m,$$
which is positive for large $n$ because the $r_m$ grow. For this curve $r_1 = 2$ and $r_2 = 1$, since $U_1 = \mathbb{Q}_p(1)^2$ and $U^2/U^3 = \mathbb{Q}_p(2)$ [@bdckw2018, §6.1, p. 43]. So:

- For $S = \emptyset$, level $n = 1$ suffices, which fits $\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{Z}) = \emptyset$.
- For $|S| \ge 1$, the count never succeeds at level $1$, since $2|S| \ge 2 = \dim [\pi_{1,DR}]_1$. Level $1$ sees only the abelianization, and the inequality $2|S| \ge 2$ plays the role of a Mordell–Weil rank at least the genus, where classical Chabauty fails. This is why the nonabelian levels are needed.
- For $|S| = 1$, level $n = 2$ suffices, since the bound is $2$ and $\dim [\pi_{1,DR}]_2 = 3$. With Kim's condition $(p-1)/2 \ge 3$, this needs $p \ge 7$.

**Context.** Kim notes that there is apparently a compactness argument that derives the theorems of Siegel and Faltings directly from the [section conjecture](section-conjecture.md), and presents his method as a different route from the fundamental group to Diophantine finiteness [@kim2005, p. 1]. Refining the Selmer variety by local conditions at the primes $v \ne p$ leads to [Kim's non-abelian Tate–Shafarevich conjecture](kim-nonabelian-tate-shafarevich-conjecture.md), which predicts exactly which $p$-adic points survive.
