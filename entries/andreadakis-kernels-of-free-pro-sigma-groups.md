**Setting.** Let $\Sigma$ be a set of primes with at least two elements, and let $F$ be a free pro-$\Sigma$ group with basis $x_1, \dots, x_n$, where $n \ge 2$. Notation and inputs 1–3 are as in [rival copies of free pro-Σ groups](rival-copies-of-free-pro-sigma-groups-in-their-automorphism-groups.md), Setting. By part 2 there, $F$ has trivial centre, so conjugation identifies $F$ with the normal subgroup $\mathrm{Inn}(F)$ of $\mathrm{Aut}(F)$.

- *Lower central series.* $F_1 = F$, and $F_{i+1}$ is the closed subgroup generated by the commutators $[a, b]$ with $a \in F_i$ and $b \in F$. It is characteristic, and the Andreadakis kernel $K_i(F)$ is the kernel of $\mathrm{Aut}(F) \to \mathrm{Aut}(F/F_{i+1})$.
- *Pro-$\ell$ quotients.* For $\ell \in \Sigma$, let $\pi_\ell : F \to F(\ell)$ be the continuous homomorphism onto a free pro-$\ell$ group with basis $\bar x_1, \dots, \bar x_n$ and $\pi_\ell(x_j) = \bar x_j$. It exists by the universal property of $F$, and it is onto because its image is closed and contains a basis. Every continuous homomorphism $h$ from $F$ to a pro-$\ell$ group $P$ factors through $\pi_\ell$: the homomorphism $h' : F(\ell) \to P$ with $h'(\bar x_j) = h(x_j)$ satisfies $h' \circ \pi_\ell = h$, as both agree on the basis.
- *Profinite powers.* Write $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma = \prod_{\ell \in \Sigma} \mathbb{Z}_\ell$, and $\nu_\ell$ for the $\ell$-component of $\nu \in \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma$. For $z$ in a pro-$\Sigma$ group, $\nu \mapsto z^\nu$ is the continuous homomorphism $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma \to \overline{\langle z \rangle}$ extending $k \mapsto z^k$. Continuous homomorphisms preserve these powers, and $(z^\nu)^\mu = z^{\nu\mu}$. If $z$ lies in a pro-$\ell$ group, then $z^\nu = z^{\nu_\ell}$, because the factor $\prod_{\ell' \ne \ell} \mathbb{Z}_{\ell'}$ has no nontrivial pro-$\ell$ quotient. If $z$ has finite order $d$, then $\nu \mapsto z^\nu$ factors through $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma \to \mathbb{Z}/d$, so $z^\nu = z^k$ for any integer $k$ with the same image in $\mathbb{Z}/d$ as $\nu$.

**Theorem.** This is proved here. Choose distinct primes $p, q \in \Sigma$ with $p$ odd, which is possible because $\Sigma$ has two elements. Let $\lambda \in \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma$ have $p$-component $-1$ and all other components $1$, and let $f \in \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma$ have $q$-component $1$ and all other components $0$. Then the continuous endomorphism $\gamma$ of $F$ with
$$\gamma(x_1) = (x_1^{\lambda} x_2^{f})^{\lambda}\, x_2^{-f}, \qquad \gamma(x_j) = x_j \quad (2 \le j \le n)$$
is an automorphism of $F$, and

1. $\pi_\ell \circ \gamma = \pi_\ell$ for every $\ell \in \Sigma$;
2. $\gamma \in K_i(F)$ for every $i \ge 1$;
3. $\gamma$ is not inner.

Consequently $\bigcap_i F K_i(F) \ne F$ in $\mathrm{Aut}(F)$.

**Why it matters.** Tsujimura's proof for $\Sigma = \{p\}$ locates a rival normal copy of $F$ inside $F K_i(F)$ for every $i$ ([free pro-$p$ groups](free-pro-p-groups-inside-their-automorphism-groups.md), Method and scope). For this to place the copy inside $F$, the subgroups $F K_i(F)$ must meet in $F$. The theorem shows that for two or more primes, and every rank $n \ge 2$, they do not: knowing that a closed subgroup lies in every $F K_i(F)$ does not place it inside $F$. So that step cannot by itself answer [the question for free pro-Σ groups](free-pro-sigma-groups-inside-their-automorphism-groups.md). This is a statement about the method only. It does not show that a rival copy exists.

The statement was not found in the sources opened: Tsujimura reports no partial answers to his question [@tsujimura2026freeaut, Introduction, p. 2].

**Proof that $\gamma$ is an automorphism.** Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be the continuous endomorphisms of $F$ that fix $x_2, \dots, x_n$ and have $\alpha(x_1) = x_1^\lambda$ and $\beta(x_1) = x_1x_2^f$. As $\lambda^2 = 1$, the endomorphism $\alpha \circ \alpha$ fixes every $x_j$, so it is the identity. Let $\beta'$ fix $x_2, \dots, x_n$ and have $\beta'(x_1) = x_1x_2^{-f}$. Then $\beta \circ \beta'$ and $\beta' \circ \beta$ fix every $x_j$, since $\beta$ and $\beta'$ fix $x_2^{\pm f}$, so both are the identity. Hence $\alpha, \beta \in \mathrm{Aut}(F)$, with $\alpha^{-1} = \alpha$ and $\beta^{-1} = \beta'$. Applying $\beta^{-1}$, $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\alpha$ in turn to $x_1$ gives
$$x_1 \mapsto x_1x_2^{-f} \mapsto x_1^\lambda x_2^{-f} \mapsto (x_1x_2^f)^\lambda x_2^{-f} \mapsto (x_1^\lambda x_2^f)^\lambda x_2^{-f},$$
and all four maps fix $x_2, \dots, x_n$. So $\gamma = \alpha\beta\alpha\beta^{-1}$ is an automorphism.

**Proof of part 1.** Both sides are continuous homomorphisms $F \to F(\ell)$, so it suffices to compare them on $x_1$. In the pro-$\ell$ group $F(\ell)$, powers depend only on $\ell$-components. If $\ell \ne p$, then $\lambda_\ell = 1$, so $\pi_\ell(\gamma(x_1)) = \bar x_1 \bar x_2^{f_\ell} \bar x_2^{-f_\ell} = \bar x_1$. If $\ell = p$, then $\lambda_p = -1$ and $f_p = 0$, so $\pi_p(\gamma(x_1)) = (\bar x_1^{-1})^{-1} = \bar x_1$. $\square$

**Proof of part 2.** Fix $i$. Since $\gamma$ fixes $x_2, \dots, x_n$, it suffices to show that $\gamma(x_1)x_1^{-1} \in F_{i+1}$: then the automorphism that $\gamma$ induces on $F/F_{i+1}$ fixes a set of topological generators, so it is the identity. The closed normal subgroup $F_{i+1}$ is the intersection of the open normal subgroups $U$ containing it. Fix such a $U$, and let $P = F/U$. The image of $F_{i+1}$ in the finite group $P$ is the $(i+1)$-st term of the lower central series of $P$, so $P$ is nilpotent, and is therefore the direct product of its Sylow subgroups $P_\ell$, for primes $\ell \in \Sigma$. For each $\ell$, the composite $F \to P \to P_\ell$ factors through $\pi_\ell$, so by part 1 it takes the same value on $\gamma(x_1)$ and on $x_1$. Hence $\gamma(x_1)$ and $x_1$ have the same image in $P$, that is, $\gamma(x_1)x_1^{-1} \in U$. $\square$

**$\gamma$ is not the identity.** Since $p \ne q$, there is a primitive $p$-th root of unity $\zeta$ in a finite extension of $\mathbb{F}_q$. Let $V = \mathbb{F}_q(\zeta)$, and let $G = V \rtimes \langle \zeta \rangle$ with multiplication $(v, s)(w, t) = (v + sw, st)$, so that $(v, s)^{-1} = (-s^{-1}v, s^{-1})$. Its order is $|V| \cdot p$, a product of primes in $\Sigma$, so there is a continuous homomorphism $\rho : F \to G$ with $\rho(x_1) = X = (0, \zeta)$, $\rho(x_2) = Y = (1, 1)$ and $\rho(x_j) = 1$ for $j \ge 3$. Here $X$ has order $p$ and $Y$ has order $q$, so $X^\lambda = X^{-1} = (0, \zeta^{-1})$, $Y^f = Y$ and $Y^{-f} = Y^{-1} = (-1, 1)$. Then $Z = X^\lambda Y^f = (\zeta^{-1}, \zeta^{-1})$. By induction $Z^k = (\zeta^{-1}(1 + \zeta^{-1} + \dots + \zeta^{-(k-1)}), \zeta^{-k})$, and $1 + \zeta^{-1} + \dots + \zeta^{-(p-1)} = 0$ because $\zeta^{-1} \ne 1$ is a root of $x^p - 1 = (x - 1)(1 + x + \dots + x^{p-1})$. So $Z^p = 1$, and $Z^\lambda = Z^{-1} = (-1, \zeta)$. Therefore
$$\rho(\gamma(x_1)) = Z^{-1}Y^{-1} = (-1, \zeta)(-1, 1) = (-1 - \zeta, \zeta),$$
while $\rho(x_1) = (0, \zeta)$. As $p$ is odd, $\zeta \ne -1$, so $\gamma(x_1) \ne x_1$.

**Proof of part 3.** Suppose that $\gamma(z) = gzg^{-1}$ for some $g \in F$ and all $z \in F$. Then $g \ne 1$, as $\gamma(x_1) \ne x_1$, and $g$ commutes with $x_2$, as $\gamma(x_2) = x_2$. For each $\ell \in \Sigma$, part 1 gives $\pi_\ell(g)\pi_\ell(z)\pi_\ell(g)^{-1} = \pi_\ell(z)$ for all $z \in F$. As $\pi_\ell$ is onto, $\pi_\ell(g)$ is central in $F(\ell)$, hence trivial by part 2 of [rival copies](rival-copies-of-free-pro-sigma-groups-in-their-automorphism-groups.md) applied with $\Sigma = \{\ell\}$. So $g \in \ker \pi_\ell$ for every $\ell \in \Sigma$.

*A prime.* For $\ell \in \Sigma$ let $e_\ell \in \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma$ have $\ell$-component $1$ and all others $0$. If $g^{e_\ell} = 1$ for every $\ell$, then $g^{e_S} = \prod_{\ell \in S} g^{e_\ell} = 1$ for every finite $S \subseteq \Sigma$, where $e_S = \sum_{\ell \in S} e_\ell$. As $e_S \to 1$ in $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma$, continuity gives $g = 1$, which is false. So fix $\ell \in \Sigma$ with $c = g^{e_\ell} \ne 1$, and put $d = x_2^{e_\ell}$. Let $C$ and $D$ be the closed subgroups generated by $c$ and $d$. They are pro-$\ell$, since $\nu \mapsto c^\nu$ and $\nu \mapsto d^\nu$ kill $(1 - e_\ell)\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma$, so both groups are quotients of $e_\ell\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma \cong \mathbb{Z}_\ell$.

*A direct product.* The centralizer of $x_2$ is a closed subgroup containing $g$, so it contains $C$. Hence every element of $C$ commutes with $x_2$, so with $d$, so with $D$. Let $a : F \to \mathbb{Z}_\ell^n$ be the continuous homomorphism sending $x_j$ to the $j$-th standard basis vector $\varepsilon_j$ (input 3). Then $a(d^\nu) = \nu\varepsilon_2$ for $\nu \in \mathbb{Z}_\ell$, so the surjection $\mathbb{Z}_\ell \to D$, $\nu \mapsto d^\nu$, is injective, and $a$ is injective on $D$. As $\mathbb{Z}_\ell^n$ is pro-$\ell$, $a$ factors through $\pi_\ell$, so $C \subseteq \ker \pi_\ell \subseteq \ker a$, and $C \cap D = 1$. So multiplication $D \times C \to W = DC$ is a continuous bijective homomorphism of compact groups, hence an isomorphism, and $W$ is a closed subgroup of $F$.

*A contradiction.* As $C \ne 1$ is pro-$\ell$, it has an open normal subgroup of index $\ell$, hence a continuous surjection $s : C \to \mathbb{Z}/\ell$. Then $s(c) \ne 0$, because $c$ generates a dense subgroup, and we may scale $s$ so that $s(c) = 1$. Let $t, \varphi : W \to \mathbb{Z}/\ell$ be the continuous homomorphisms $t(yx) = s(x)$ and $\varphi(yx) = \bar\nu$ for $y = d^\nu \in D$ and $x \in C$, where $\bar\nu$ is $\nu$ modulo $\ell$. So $t(c) = 1$, $t(d) = 0$, $\varphi(d) = 1$ and $\varphi(c) = 0$. The function $(x, y) \mapsto t(x)\varphi(y)$ is a continuous $2$-cocycle with values in $\mathbb{Z}/\ell$ with trivial action, and $H^2(W, \mathbb{Z}/\ell) = 0$ by input 2. So there is a continuous $\eta : W \to \mathbb{Z}/\ell$ with $t(x)\varphi(y) = \eta(x) + \eta(y) - \eta(xy)$ for all $x, y \in W$. The pairs $(c, d)$ and $(d, c)$ give $1 = \eta(c) + \eta(d) - \eta(cd)$ and $0 = \eta(d) + \eta(c) - \eta(dc)$. As $cd = dc$, subtracting gives $1 = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/\ell$. So $\gamma$ is not inner. $\square$

**Proof of the consequence.** By part 2, $\gamma \in K_i(F) \subseteq FK_i(F)$ for every $i$, and by part 3, $\gamma \notin F$. $\square$

**Remarks.** Some inner automorphisms also lie in every $K_i(F)$: conjugation by an element of $\bigcap_i F_i$. This intersection is nontrivial. Indeed $\rho$ maps $F$ onto $G$, since $\rho(F)$ contains $X$ and the elements $X^kYX^{-k} = (\zeta^k, 1)$, which span $V$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$. And $G$ is not nilpotent, because its centre is trivial: an element $(v, s)$ commuting with $(1, 1)$ has $s = 1$, and $(v, 1)$ commutes with $X$ only if $\zeta v = v$, that is, $v = 0$. So no $F_i$ lies in the open subgroup $\ker\rho$, and by compactness the decreasing closed subgroups $F_i$ do not meet in $1$. But these automorphisms lie in $F$, so on their own they say nothing about the method. For $\Sigma = \{p\}$ the conclusion fails: a pro-$p$ group has $\bigcap_i F_i = 1$, because its finite quotients are nilpotent, so the $K_i(F)$ have trivial intersection.
