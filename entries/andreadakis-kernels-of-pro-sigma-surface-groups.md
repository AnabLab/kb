**Setting.** Let $\Sigma$ be a set of primes with at least two elements, and let $g \ge 2$. Write $[a, b] = aba^{-1}b^{-1}$, and let $\Pi$ be the pro-$\Sigma$ group on $x_1, y_1, \dots, x_g, y_g$ with the single relation $[x_1, y_1] \cdots [x_g, y_g] = 1$. By definition of a pro-$\Sigma$ presentation, every tuple $(X_1, Y_1, \dots, X_g, Y_g)$ in a pro-$\Sigma$ group $H$ with $[X_1, Y_1] \cdots [X_g, Y_g] = 1$ is the image of the generators under exactly one continuous homomorphism $\Pi \to H$. Two continuous homomorphisms that agree on the generators are equal. When $\Sigma$ is the set of all primes, $\Pi$ is the profinite surface group $\widehat{\Gamma}_{g,0}$ of [Tsujimura's question](profinite-surface-groups-inside-their-automorphism-groups.md). With the other convention $a^{-1}b^{-1}ab$ for commutators, the substitution $x_i \mapsto x_i^{-1}$, $y_i \mapsto y_i^{-1}$ identifies the two presentations, because $a^{-1}b^{-1}ab = [a^{-1}, b^{-1}]$. Automorphisms are continuous.

- *Profinite powers.* Put $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma = \prod_{\ell \in \Sigma} \mathbb{Z}_\ell$, write $\nu_\ell$ for the $\ell$-component of $\nu \in \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma$, and let $e_\ell$ have $\ell$-component $1$ and all other components $0$. For $z$ in a pro-$\Sigma$ group, $\nu \mapsto z^\nu$ is the continuous homomorphism $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma \to \overline{\langle z \rangle}$ extending $k \mapsto z^k$. Continuous homomorphisms preserve these powers. If $z$ lies in a pro-$\ell$ group, then $z^\nu = z^{\nu_\ell}$. If $z$ has finite order $d$, then $z^\nu = z^k$ for every integer $k$ with the same image as $\nu$ in $\mathbb{Z}/d$.
- *Maximal pro-$\ell$ quotients.* For $\ell \in \Sigma$, let $R_\ell$ be the intersection of the open normal subgroups of $\Pi$ of $\ell$-power index, $\Pi(\ell) = \Pi/R_\ell$, and $\pi_\ell : \Pi \to \Pi(\ell)$ the projection. The kernel of a continuous homomorphism from $\Pi$ to a pro-$\ell$ group is an intersection of open normal subgroups of $\ell$-power index, so it contains $R_\ell$, and the homomorphism factors through $\pi_\ell$. Automorphisms of $\Pi$ permute the open normal subgroups of $\ell$-power index, so they preserve $R_\ell$, and each $\alpha \in \mathrm{Aut}(\Pi)$ induces $\alpha_\ell \in \mathrm{Aut}(\Pi(\ell))$ with $\pi_\ell \circ \alpha = \alpha_\ell \circ \pi_\ell$. Moreover $\Pi(\ell)$, with the images of the generators, is the pro-$\ell$ group with the same presentation, the pro-$\ell$ surface group of genus $g$, because both have the same continuous homomorphisms to pro-$\ell$ groups.
- *Lower central series.* Let $\Pi_1 = \Pi$, and let $\Pi_{i+1}$ be the closed subgroup generated by the $[a, b]$ with $a \in \Pi_i$ and $b \in \Pi$. These subgroups are characteristic. The Andreadakis kernel $K_i(\Pi)$ is the kernel of $\mathrm{Aut}(\Pi) \to \mathrm{Aut}(\Pi/\Pi_{i+1})$, and $\mathrm{Inn}(\Pi)$ is the group of inner automorphisms.

**Theorem.** This is proved here. Choose distinct primes $p, q \in \Sigma$. For $\nu \in \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma$, let $A_\nu$ and $B_\nu$ be the endomorphisms of $\Pi$ that fix $x_2, y_2, \dots, x_g, y_g$ and satisfy
$$A_\nu(x_1) = x_1, \quad A_\nu(y_1) = y_1x_1^\nu, \qquad B_\nu(x_1) = x_1y_1^\nu, \quad B_\nu(y_1) = y_1.$$
They are automorphisms, and $\gamma = A_{e_p} B_{e_q} A_{e_p}^{-1} B_{e_q}^{-1}$ satisfies:

1. $\pi_\ell \circ \gamma = \pi_\ell$ for every $\ell \in \Sigma$;
2. $\gamma \in K_i(\Pi)$ for every $i \ge 1$;
3. $\gamma$ is not inner.

Consequently, the automorphisms acting trivially on every $\Pi(\ell)$ do not all lie in $\mathrm{Inn}(\Pi)$, and $\bigcap_i \mathrm{Inn}(\Pi)K_i(\Pi) \ne \mathrm{Inn}(\Pi)$.

**Why it matters.** For the pro-$p$ surface group $D$ of genus $g \ge 2$, the proof that $D$ is the only closed normal subgroup of $\mathrm{Aut}(D)$ isomorphic to $D$ places such a subgroup in $DK_i(D)$ for every $i$, and then uses $\bigcap_i DK_i(D) = D$ ([pro-$p$ surface groups](pro-p-surface-groups-inside-their-automorphism-groups.md), Steps 6 and 7; [lower central series](lower-central-series-of-pro-p-surface-groups.md), part 4(d)). It follows Tsujimura's proof for free pro-$p$ groups ([free pro-$p$ groups](free-pro-p-groups-inside-their-automorphism-groups.md), Method and scope). For the profinite surface group the theorem shows that the subgroups $\mathrm{Inn}(\Pi)K_i(\Pi)$ meet in more than $\mathrm{Inn}(\Pi)$. So knowing that a closed normal copy of $\Pi$ lies in all of them would not place it inside $\mathrm{Inn}(\Pi)$, and this step cannot by itself answer [Tsujimura's question](profinite-surface-groups-inside-their-automorphism-groups.md). The first consequence says the same of a second route: knowing that a closed subgroup of $\mathrm{Aut}(\Pi)$ acts on every pro-$\ell$ surface group $\Pi(\ell)$ by inner automorphisms, for instance by applying the pro-$\ell$ theorem prime by prime, would not place it inside $\mathrm{Inn}(\Pi)$ either. These are statements about methods only. They do not show that a rival copy exists.

**Proof that $A_\nu$ and $B_\nu$ are automorphisms.** As $x_1^\nu$ commutes with $x_1$,
$$[x_1, y_1x_1^\nu] = x_1 \cdot y_1x_1^\nu \cdot x_1^{-1} \cdot x_1^{-\nu}y_1^{-1} = x_1y_1x_1^{-1}y_1^{-1} = [x_1, y_1],$$
and similarly $[x_1y_1^\nu, y_1] = x_1y_1^\nu \cdot y_1 \cdot y_1^{-\nu}x_1^{-1} \cdot y_1^{-1} = [x_1, y_1]$. So the images of the generators satisfy the relation, and $A_\nu$, $B_\nu$ are well-defined continuous endomorphisms. Since $A_\nu(x_1^\mu) = x_1^\mu$, we get $A_\nu(A_\mu(y_1)) = y_1x_1^{\nu}x_1^{\mu} = y_1x_1^{\nu+\mu}$, so $A_\nu A_\mu = A_{\nu+\mu}$, as both sides agree on the generators. Likewise $B_\nu B_\mu = B_{\nu+\mu}$. As $A_0 = B_0 = \mathrm{id}$, each $A_\nu$ and $B_\nu$ is an automorphism, with inverse $A_{-\nu}$ or $B_{-\nu}$. $\square$

**Proof of part 1.** Let $\ell \in \Sigma$ and $\nu \in \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma$ with $\nu_\ell = 0$. In the pro-$\ell$ group $\Pi(\ell)$, $\pi_\ell(x_1)^\nu = \pi_\ell(x_1)^{\nu_\ell} = 1$, so $\pi_\ell \circ A_\nu$ agrees with $\pi_\ell$ on the generators, and $\pi_\ell \circ A_\nu = \pi_\ell$. Similarly $\pi_\ell \circ B_\nu = \pi_\ell$. As $(\pm e_p)_\ell = 0$ for $\ell \ne p$ and $(\pm e_q)_\ell = 0$ for $\ell \ne q$, this gives $\pi_\ell \circ A_{e_p}^{\pm 1} = \pi_\ell$ when $\ell \ne p$, and $\pi_\ell \circ B_{e_q}^{\pm 1} = \pi_\ell$ when $\ell \ne q$. Write $A = A_{e_p}$ and $B = B_{e_q}$, and let $\beta = B_\ell$ and $\alpha = A_\ell$ be the induced automorphisms of $\Pi(\ell)$, so that $\pi_\ell \circ B^{\pm 1} = \beta^{\pm 1} \circ \pi_\ell$ and $\pi_\ell \circ A^{\pm 1} = \alpha^{\pm 1} \circ \pi_\ell$. If $\ell \ne p$, then
$$\pi_\ell \circ ABA^{-1}B^{-1} = \pi_\ell \circ BA^{-1}B^{-1} = \beta \circ \pi_\ell \circ A^{-1}B^{-1} = \beta \circ \pi_\ell \circ B^{-1} = \beta\beta^{-1} \circ \pi_\ell = \pi_\ell.$$
If $\ell = p$, then $\ell \ne q$, and
$$\pi_\ell \circ ABA^{-1}B^{-1} = \alpha \circ \pi_\ell \circ BA^{-1}B^{-1} = \alpha \circ \pi_\ell \circ A^{-1}B^{-1} = \alpha\alpha^{-1} \circ \pi_\ell \circ B^{-1} = \pi_\ell. \qquad \square$$

**Proof of part 2.** Fix $i$. It suffices to show that $\gamma(z)z^{-1} \in \Pi_{i+1}$ for each generator $z$: then the automorphism that $\gamma$ induces on $\Pi/\Pi_{i+1}$ fixes a set of topological generators, so it is the identity. The closed normal subgroup $\Pi_{i+1}$ is the intersection of the open normal subgroups $U$ containing it. Fix such a $U$ and put $P = \Pi/U$. The image of $\Pi_{i+1}$ in the finite group $P$ is the $(i+1)$-st term of the lower central series of $P$, so $P$ is nilpotent, and is therefore the direct product of its Sylow subgroups $P_\ell$, for primes $\ell \in \Sigma$. For each $\ell$, the composite $\Pi \to P \to P_\ell$ factors through $\pi_\ell$, so by part 1 it takes the same value on $\gamma(z)$ and on $z$. Hence $\gamma(z)$ and $z$ have the same image in $P$, that is, $\gamma(z)z^{-1} \in U$. $\square$

**Proof of part 3.** *A finite quotient.* Since $p \ne q$, the polynomial $x^p - 1$ is separable over $\mathbb{F}_q$, so a finite extension $V = \mathbb{F}_q(\zeta)$ of $\mathbb{F}_q$ contains a primitive $p$-th root of unity $\zeta$. Let $G = V \rtimes \langle\zeta\rangle$, with multiplication $(v, s)(w, t) = (v + sw, st)$, identity $(0, 1)$ and inverses $(v, s)^{-1} = (-s^{-1}v, s^{-1})$. Its order is $|V| \cdot p$, a product of powers of $p$ and $q$, so $G$ is a finite $\Sigma$-group. Put $X = (0, \zeta)$ and $Y = (1, 1)$.

- *Orders.* If $s \ne 1$, then $(v, s)^k = (v(1 + s + \dots + s^{k-1}), s^k)$, and $1 + s + \dots + s^{p-1} = 0$ because $s$ is a root of $x^p - 1 = (x - 1)(1 + x + \dots + x^{p-1})$ other than $1$. So $(v, s)$ has order $p$. If $v \ne 0$, then $(v, 1)$ has order $q$, as $V$ has characteristic $q$. Now $e_p$ has image $1$ in $\mathbb{Z}/p$ and $0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/q$, and $e_q$ the reverse. So for $z \in G$ of order $p$, $z^{\pm e_p} = z^{\pm 1}$ and $z^{\pm e_q} = 1$, and for $z$ of order $q$, $z^{\pm e_q} = z^{\pm 1}$ and $z^{\pm e_p} = 1$.
- *Generation.* $X^kYX^{-k} = (\zeta^k, 1)$, and the powers of $\zeta$ span $V$ over the prime field $\mathbb{F}_q$. As the elements $(v, 1)$ form a subgroup isomorphic to $V$, the elements $X^kYX^{-k}$ generate it, and together with $X$ they generate $G$. So $G = \langle X, Y \rangle$.

As $[Y, X] = [X, Y]^{-1}$, there is a continuous homomorphism $\rho : \Pi \to G$ with $\rho(x_1) = X$, $\rho(y_1) = Y$, $\rho(x_2) = Y$, $\rho(y_2) = X$, and $\rho(x_j) = \rho(y_j) = 1$ for $j \ge 3$.

*The value of $\rho \circ \gamma$.* For a continuous homomorphism $\sigma : \Pi \to G$ and an automorphism $\phi$ given by expressions for the $\phi(z)$ in the generators, $\sigma \circ \phi$ sends each generator $z$ to the value of that expression at the elements $\sigma(x_1), \sigma(y_1), \dots$, because $\sigma$ preserves products and profinite powers. Apply this four times, using the orders above. All four maps send $x_j, y_j$ to $\rho(x_j), \rho(y_j)$ for $j \ge 2$.

- $\sigma_1 = \rho \circ A_{e_p}$: $x_1 \mapsto X$, $y_1 \mapsto YX^{e_p} = YX = (1, \zeta)$.
- $\sigma_2 = \sigma_1 \circ B_{e_q}$: $x_1 \mapsto X(1, \zeta)^{e_q} = X$, since $(1, \zeta)$ has order $p$; $y_1 \mapsto (1, \zeta)$.
- $\sigma_3 = \sigma_2 \circ A_{-e_p}$: $x_1 \mapsto X$, $y_1 \mapsto (1, \zeta)X^{-e_p} = (1, \zeta)(0, \zeta^{-1}) = (1, 1) = Y$.
- $\sigma_4 = \sigma_3 \circ B_{-e_q}$: $x_1 \mapsto XY^{-e_q} = (0, \zeta)(-1, 1) = (-\zeta, \zeta)$, $y_1 \mapsto Y$.

Here $\sigma_4 = \rho \circ A_{e_p}B_{e_q}A_{-e_p}B_{-e_q} = \rho \circ \gamma$. So $\rho(\gamma(x_1)) = (-\zeta, \zeta)$, while $\rho(x_1) = (0, \zeta)$, and these differ because $\zeta \ne 0$.

*Conclusion.* Suppose that $\gamma(z) = hzh^{-1}$ for some $h \in \Pi$ and all $z \in \Pi$. Then $\rho(\gamma(z)) = \rho(h)\rho(z)\rho(h)^{-1}$. Since $\gamma$ fixes $x_2$ and $y_2$, which exist because $g \ge 2$, the element $\rho(h)$ commutes with $\rho(x_2) = Y$ and $\rho(y_2) = X$. As $G = \langle X, Y \rangle$, it is central in $G$, so $\rho(\gamma(x_1)) = \rho(x_1)$. This contradicts the computation, so $\gamma$ is not inner. $\square$

**Proof of the consequences.** By part 1, $\gamma_\ell = \mathrm{id}$ for every $\ell \in \Sigma$, and by part 3, $\gamma \notin \mathrm{Inn}(\Pi)$. By part 2, $\gamma \in K_i(\Pi) \subseteq \mathrm{Inn}(\Pi)K_i(\Pi)$ for every $i$. $\square$

**Remarks.**

- *One prime.* For $\Sigma = \{p\}$ the conclusion fails: $\bigcap_i DK_i(D) = D$ for the pro-$p$ surface group $D$ ([lower central series](lower-central-series-of-pro-p-surface-groups.md), part 4(d)). The proof above needs two distinct primes $p$ and $q$ at two places, in part 1, so that every $\ell$ differs from $p$ or from $q$, and in the finite group $G$, which is not nilpotent.
- *Where $\gamma$ comes from.* For integers $\nu$, the same substitutions and the same computation give automorphisms of the discrete group with the same presentation, the fundamental group of a closed orientable surface of genus $g$. The map $\nu \mapsto A_\nu$ is a homomorphism $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma \to \mathrm{Aut}(\Pi)$, and it is continuous: for a characteristic open subgroup $U$ of $\Pi$, $A_\nu$ acts trivially on $\Pi/U$ exactly when $x_1^\nu \in U$, which holds on an open subgroup of $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma$. The same holds for $\nu \mapsto B_\nu$. As $\mathbb{Z}$ is dense in $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_\Sigma$, $\gamma$ lies in the closed subgroup of $\mathrm{Aut}(\Pi)$ topologically generated by $A_1$ and $B_1$.
- *Sources.* The argument is self-contained, and no source for the statement is known here. [Tsujimura's question](profinite-surface-groups-inside-their-automorphism-groups.md) records that he reports no partial answers.
