**Setting.** Use the notation of [induced subgraphs on $n-1$ vertices decide the property](small-induced-subgraphs-decide-the-kernel-unipotent-property.md): a prime $p$, a finite field $\mathbb F$ of characteristic $p$, a finite simple graph $\Gamma=(V,E)$ with right-angled Artin pro-$p$ group $G=G_\Gamma$, the groups $G_S$ and maps $\sigma_S,\tau_S,e_S$ for $S\subseteq V$, the graded algebra $A(H)$, initial forms $\mathrm{in}_k$, corner functionals $\kappa_\rho$ and formula (1), the free algebra $T=\mathbb F_p\langle X_v:v\in V\rangle$ with $\mu:T\to A(G)$, the spaces $\mathrm{Lie}_R$, and its Lemmas 1–5. Jennings' theorem is used as recalled in that Setting. For $S\subseteq V$, $\mu_S:T(S)\to A(G_S)$ denotes the same map for the graph $\Gamma[S]$, where $T(S)\subseteq T$ is the free algebra on the $X_v$ with $v\in S$; lemmas stated for $\Gamma$ are applied to $\Gamma[S]$ in this way.

*Words.* A word is a finite sequence of vertices; letters may repeat. Its multidegree $\alpha\in\mathbb N^V$ counts the occurrences of each vertex, and its support is the set of vertices that occur. For $Q\in T$ write $\langle Q,X_w\rangle$ for the coefficient of $X_w$, $T_k$ for the span of the words of length $k$, $T_\alpha$ for the span of the words of multidegree $\alpha$, and $Q_\alpha$ for the component of $Q$ in $T_\alpha$. For $S\subseteq V$, a word is *$S$-multilinear* if each vertex of $S$ occurs once and no other vertex occurs. Two words are *equivalent* if one is obtained from the other by repeatedly exchanging two consecutive letters that are distinct adjacent vertices; the equivalence classes are called *classes*. For a class $t$ put $c_t(Q)=\sum_{w\in t}\langle Q,X_w\rangle$. By Lemma 4(b), $\mu(X_w)$ depends only on the class of $w$. Exchanges never swap two non-adjacent letters, so we have the *pair invariant*: for distinct non-adjacent vertices $a,b$, all words of a class have the same subsequence of letters equal to $a$ or $b$. Deleting from equivalent words every occurrence of a vertex gives equivalent words.

A *block word* is a concatenation $w=B_1B_2\cdots B_k$ of nonempty words such that the letters of each $B_i$ are distinct and pairwise adjacent, and no letter of $B_i$ is adjacent to a letter of $B_{i+1}$. A *chain word* is a block word whose blocks have length $1$: its consecutive letters are equal or non-adjacent. Lemma 5 concerns $S$-multilinear chain words.

*Brackets.* Let $\mathcal L\subseteq T$ be the Lie subalgebra generated by the $X_v$ under $[P,Q]=PQ-QP$. It is spanned by the *brackets*, the iterated commutators of the $X_v$ with any bracketing and repetitions allowed, and each bracket lies in some $T_\alpha$. For a vertex $z$ and a word $v=(v_1,\ldots,v_m)$ put
$$\ell_v=[\cdots[[X_z,X_{v_1}],X_{v_2}],\ldots,X_{v_m}],$$
with $z$ understood from the context.

**Theorem.** This is proved here.

1. *Degree six.* For every finite simple graph $\Gamma$, every prime $p$ and every finite field $\mathbb F$ of characteristic $p$, $G_\Gamma$ has the kernel $6$-unipotent property over $\mathbb F$. With part 3 of the induced-subgraph theorem, it has the kernel $n$-unipotent property over $\mathbb F$ for every $n\le6$.
2. *Elements with $d$-vertex support in degree $d$.* Let $d\ge1$, let $g\in G_{(d)}\setminus G_{(d+1)}$, and let $S\subseteq V$ be a set of least size with $e_S(g)\notin G_{(d+1)}$. Suppose that $|S|=d$, and that $\Gamma[S]$ has a vertex lying on every induced $3$-vertex path of $\Gamma[S]$, or a vertex $z$ with exactly one non-neighbour $u$ in $S$ such that $u$ lies on every induced $3$-vertex path of $\Gamma[S\setminus\{z\}]$. Then $\rho(g)\ne1$ for some continuous $\rho:G\to U_{d+1}(\mathbb F_p)$.

In part 2 the first condition holds vacuously when $\Gamma[S]$ has no induced $3$-vertex path, that is, when every component of $\Gamma[S]$ is complete.

**Corollaries.** These are proved here.

- (a) *The first failing degree.* In the notation of corollary (c) of the induced-subgraph theorem, $7\le n_{\min}(p)\le n_0$ for every prime $p$. By corollary (a) of that theorem, the kernel $7$-unipotent property over $\mathbb F$ holds for every finite graph exactly when it holds for the graphs on six vertices.
- (b) *Galois groups.* If $K$ contains a primitive $p$th root of unity and $G_K(p)\cong G_\Gamma$ for a finite graph $\Gamma$, then $G_K(p)$ satisfies the equality of the [kernel-unipotent conjecture](kernel-unipotent-conjecture.md) for every $n\le6$. This applies, for example, to $(\mathbb Z_p\times F_r)*\mathbb Z_p$ with $r\ge2$, as in corollary (b) of the induced-subgraph theorem.

**Lemma A (restricted brackets).** Let $W\subseteq T$ be the span of the elements $m^{p^j}$ with $m$ a bracket and $j\ge0$.

- (a) $[W,W]\subseteq\mathcal L\subseteq W$, and $w^p\in W$ for every $w\in W$.
- (b) For $k\ge1$ and $h\in G_{(k)}$, $\mathrm{in}_k(h)=\mu(P)$ for some $P\in W\cap T_k$.
- (c) If $P\in W$ and some entry of $\alpha$ is not divisible by $p$, then $P_\alpha$ is a linear combination of brackets of multidegree $\alpha$.

*Proof.* Write $\mathrm{ad}(x)(y)=xy-yx$ and $\mathrm{ad}(x)=L_x-R_x$ with $L_x,R_x$ the commuting left and right multiplications. In characteristic $p$ this gives $\mathrm{ad}(x)^p=L_x^p-R_x^p=\mathrm{ad}(x^p)$, hence $\mathrm{ad}(x^{p^j})=\mathrm{ad}(x)^{p^j}$. Also $\mathrm{ad}(x)^{p-1}=\sum_i\binom{p-1}iL_x^i(-R_x)^{p-1-i}$ and $\binom{p-1}i(-1)^{p-1-i}\equiv(-1)^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$, so for every derivation $D$ of an associative $\mathbb F_p$-algebra
$$D(x^p)=\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}x^iD(x)x^{p-1-i}=\mathrm{ad}(x)^{p-1}(D(x)).$$

(a) Brackets are the case $j=0$, so $\mathcal L\subseteq W$. Let $m,m'$ be brackets and $i,j\ge0$. We show $[m^{p^i},m'^{p^j}]\in\mathcal L$ by induction on $j$. For $j=0$ it equals $\mathrm{ad}(m)^{p^i}(m')$, and $\mathrm{ad}(m)$ preserves $\mathcal L$. For $j\ge1$ put $x=m'^{p^{j-1}}$. The display with $D=\mathrm{ad}(m^{p^i})$ gives $[m^{p^i},x^p]=\mathrm{ad}(x)^{p-1}([m^{p^i},x])$. Here $[m^{p^i},x]\in\mathcal L$ by induction, and $\mathrm{ad}(x)=\mathrm{ad}(m')^{p^{j-1}}$ preserves $\mathcal L$. By bilinearity $[W,W]\subseteq\mathcal L$. Next let $a,b\in W$. For $0<i<p$ let $s_i$ be the sum of the $\binom pi$ products $y_1\cdots y_p$ with $y_l\in\{a,b\}$ and exactly $i$ factors equal to $a$, so that $(a+b)^p=a^p+b^p+\sum_{0<i<p}s_i$. Then $i!(p-i)!\,s_i$ is the left side of Lemma 1 with $z_1=\cdots=z_i=a$ and $z_{i+1}=\cdots=z_p=b$. Its right side is a sum of terms $\mathrm{ad}(y_1)\cdots\mathrm{ad}(y_{p-1})(y_p)$ with $y_l\in\{a,b\}$ and $p\ge2$, which lie in $\mathcal L$ because $[W,W]\subseteq\mathcal L\subseteq W$. As $i!(p-i)!$ is prime to $p$, $(a+b)^p-a^p-b^p\in\mathcal L$. By induction on the number of terms, $(\sum_lc_lw_l)^p\equiv\sum_lc_lw_l^p\pmod{\mathcal L}$ for $c_l\in\mathbb F_p$ and generators $w_l=m_l^{p^{j_l}}$, using $c^p=c$. Since $w_l^p=m_l^{p^{j_l+1}}\in W$, this lies in $W$.

(b) The induction in the proof of Lemma 2(b) of the induced-subgraph theorem applies, with $W\cap T_k$ in place of $\mathcal P_k$. For $k=1$, $\mathrm{in}_1(G)$ is spanned by the $\mu(X_v)$. For $k\ge2$ it suffices to treat the generators of $G_{(k)}$ modulo $G_{(k+1)}$ named there. For a commutator, $\mathrm{in}_k([x,y])=\mu([P,Q])$ with $P\in W\cap T_i$ and $Q\in W\cap T_j$ by induction, and $[P,Q]\in W\cap T_k$ by (a). For a $p$th power with $pm=k$, $\mathrm{in}_k(x^p)=\mu(P^p)$ with $P\in W\cap T_m$, and $P^p\in W\cap T_k$ by (a). If $pm>k$ the initial form is $0$.

(c) $W$ is spanned by its generators $m^{p^j}$, and each lies in $T_{p^j\beta}$, where $\beta$ is the multidegree of $m$. So $P_\alpha$ is a combination of the generators lying in $T_\alpha$. If some entry of $\alpha$ is not divisible by $p$, these all have $j=0$. $\square$

**Lemma B (block representations).** Let $w=B_1\cdots B_k$ be a block word of length $d$, and let $J_i$ be the set of positions occupied by $B_i$. For a vertex $v$ put $N_v=\sum_i\sum_{j\in J_i}E_{j,j+1}$, the outer sum over the $i$ such that $v$ occurs in $B_i$. There is a continuous $\rho_w:G\to U_{d+1}(\mathbb F_p)$ with $\rho_w(v)=1+N_v$ for every $v\in V$, and for every homogeneous $Q\in T$ of degree $d$,
$$\kappa_{\rho_w}(\mu(Q))=\sum_u\langle Q,X_u\rangle,$$
the sum over the words $u$ of length $d$ such that, for every $j$, the letter $u_j$ occurs in the block containing position $j$. In particular:

- (i) if $w$ is a chain word, then $\kappa_{\rho_w}(\mu(Q))=\langle Q,X_w\rangle$;
- (ii) if $w$ is $S$-multilinear for a set $S$ and $Q$ is a combination of $S$-multilinear words, then $\kappa_{\rho_w}(\mu(Q))=c_t(Q)$, where $t$ is the class of $w$.

*Proof.* Let $s\ne s'$ be adjacent vertices. A product $E_{j,j+1}E_{j',j'+1}$ is nonzero only if $j'=j+1$. If $j$ lies in a block containing $s$ and $j+1$ in a block containing $s'$, these blocks are equal, since otherwise they would be consecutive blocks with adjacent letters $s$ and $s'$. Hence $N_sN_{s'}=\sum_i\sum_{j,j+1\in J_i}E_{j,j+2}$, the outer sum over the blocks containing both $s$ and $s'$. This is symmetric in $s$ and $s'$, so $N_sN_{s'}=N_{s'}N_s$, and $\rho_w$ exists because $U_{d+1}(\mathbb F_p)$ is a finite $p$-group. Its linear extension sends $s-1$ to $N_s$. As in the proof of Lemma 5, the $(1,d+1)$ entry of $N_{u_1}\cdots N_{u_d}$ is $\prod_j(N_{u_j})_{j,j+1}$, and $(N_s)_{j,j+1}$ is $1$ if $s$ occurs in the block containing $j$ and $0$ otherwise. This gives the formula. In (i) the condition says $u=w$. In (ii), the $S$-multilinear words in the sum are those obtained from $w$ by permuting the letters within each block, and these are equivalent to $w$. Conversely, $\kappa_{\rho_w}\circ\mu$ is constant on classes and takes the value $1$ at $X_w$, so every $S$-multilinear word in $t$ lies in the sum. $\square$

**Lemma C (expansion at a simple letter).** Let $\alpha$ be a multidegree and $z$ a vertex with $\alpha_z=1$, and let $P\in\mathcal L\cap T_\alpha$. Call a class of words of multidegree $\alpha$ *$z$-initial* if all its words begin with $z$. Below, $v$ runs over the distinct words of multidegree $\alpha-e_z$, and $\ell_v$ is taken with this $z$.

- (a) $P=\sum_v\langle P,X_zX_v\rangle\,\ell_v$.
- (b) Let $w$ be a word of multidegree $\alpha$ with $z$ in position $k$, and let $m$ be the length of $v$. Then $\langle\ell_v,X_w\rangle=(-1)^{k-1}N$, where $N$ is the number of sets $B$ of positions of $v$ such that the letters of $v$ at the positions in $B$, read from right to left, are $w_1,\ldots,w_{k-1}$, and the other letters of $v$, read from left to right, are $w_{k+1},\ldots,w_{m+1}$.
- (c) The class of $zv$ is $z$-initial if and only if no word equivalent to $v$ begins with a neighbour of $z$, and then its words are the $zv'$ with $v'$ equivalent to $v$.
- (d) If $c_t(P)=0$ for every $z$-initial class $t$, then $\mu(P)=0$.

*Proof.* (b) Expanding one bracket at a time, the $i$th step multiplies by $X_{v_i}$ on the right with sign $+$ or on the left with sign $-$. So $\ell_v$ is the sum over sets $B$ of positions of $(-1)^{|B|}X_{u'}X_zX_{u''}$, where $u'$ lists the letters of $v$ at positions in $B$ from right to left and $u''$ lists the others from left to right. The term equals $X_w$ exactly when $B$ is as stated, and then $|B|=k-1$.

(a) By (b), the only word of $\ell_v$ beginning with $z$ is $zv$, with coefficient $1$: for $B\ne\emptyset$ the word begins with a letter of $v$, and $z$ does not occur in $v$. Next, $\mathcal L\cap T_\alpha$ is spanned by the brackets of multidegree $\alpha$, and each contains $X_z$ exactly once. Such a bracket is an integer combination of the $\ell_v$, by induction on its degree as in Lemma 4(a): it is $X_z$, or $[m_1,m_2]$ where, after a sign change, $X_z$ occurs in $m_2$; then $\mathrm{ad}(m_1)$ is an integer combination of products $\mathrm{ad}(X_{s_1})\cdots\mathrm{ad}(X_{s_r})$, $m_2$ is a combination of $\ell_v$'s by induction, and $\mathrm{ad}(X_s)(\ell_v)=-\ell_{vs}$. Write $P=\sum_vb_v\ell_v$. Comparing coefficients of $X_zX_v$ gives $b_v=\langle P,X_zX_v\rangle$.

(c) If $v$ is equivalent to $sv''$ with $s$ adjacent to $z$, then $zv$ is equivalent to $szv''$. Conversely, suppose a word of the class of $zv$ begins with $s\ne z$. In $zv$ the letter $z$ comes before every $s$, so the pair invariant forces $s$ to be adjacent to $z$. Deleting $z$ from that word gives a word equivalent to $v$ that begins with $s$. In the $z$-initial case every word of the class is $zv'$, and deleting $z$ shows $v'\sim v$.

(d) Put $D_s=\mathrm{ad}(\mu(X_s))$ on $A(G)$, so that $\mu(\ell_v)=(-1)^mD_{v_m}\cdots D_{v_1}(\mu(X_z))$. For adjacent $s,s'$, Lemma 4(b) and the Jacobi identity give $D_sD_{s'}-D_{s'}D_s=\mathrm{ad}([\mu(X_s),\mu(X_{s'})])=0$, and $D_s(\mu(X_z))=0$ if $s$ is adjacent to $z$. So $\mu(\ell_v)$ depends only on the class of $v$, and vanishes if some word equivalent to $v$ begins with a neighbour of $z$. Apply $\mu$ to (a) and group the $v$ by classes $\tau$. The classes with a word beginning with a neighbour of $z$ contribute $0$. For the others, the class $t$ of $zv$ is $z$-initial by (c), and the coefficient of $\mu(\ell_v)$ is $\sum_{v'\in\tau}\langle P,X_zX_{v'}\rangle=c_t(P)$, again by (c). $\square$

**Lemma D (layered orientations).** Let $S\subseteq V$ and $\Delta=\Gamma[S]$, and let $\bar\Delta$ be its complement, the graph on $S$ whose edges are the non-adjacent pairs. For an $S$-multilinear word $w$ let $O(w)$ be the orientation of $\bar\Delta$ from the earlier letter of each pair to the later one. For an acyclic orientation $O$ of $\bar\Delta$, its *layers* are $F_1$, the set of sources of $O$, and inductively $F_{i+1}$, the set of sources of the restriction of $O$ to $S\setminus(F_1\cup\cdots\cup F_i)$. Call $O$ *layered* if no vertex of $F_i$ is adjacent in $\Gamma$ to a vertex of $F_{i+1}$, for every $i$.

- (a) Two $S$-multilinear words are equivalent if and only if they have the same orientation. The class of $w$ is $z$-initial if and only if $z$ is the only source of $O(w)$.
- (b) The class of $w$ contains a block word if and only if $O(w)$ is layered.
- (c) If $z$ lies on every induced $3$-vertex path of $\Delta$ and is the only source of $O$, then $O$ is layered.
- (d) If $z$ has exactly one non-neighbour $u$ in $S$, $u$ lies on every induced $3$-vertex path of $\Delta-z$, and $z$ is the only source of $O$, then $O$ is layered.

*Proof.* (a) Exchanges swap adjacent letters only, so they preserve $O(w)$. Conversely, let $O(w)=O(w')$ and let $x$ be the first letter of $w'$. A letter before $x$ in $w$ and not adjacent to $x$ would be oriented towards $x$ in $O(w)$ but away from $x$ in $O(w')$. So every letter before $x$ in $w$ is adjacent to $x$, and exchanges move $x$ to the front. Induction on the length, after deleting $x$, finishes the proof. The same argument shows that a letter $x$ begins some word of the class of $w$ exactly when $x$ is a source of $O(w)$, which gives the second assertion.

(b) Two sources of an orientation are not joined by an edge of $\bar\Delta$, so each layer is a set of pairwise adjacent vertices. If $a\to b$ in $O$, then $b$ is not a source while $a$ remains, so $a$ lies in an earlier layer than $b$. Hence a word listing $F_1$, then $F_2$, and so on, has orientation $O$, and if $O$ is layered it is a block word with blocks the $F_i$. Conversely, let $w'=B_1\cdots B_k$ be a block word with $O(w')=O$. A letter of $B_1$ precedes all its non-neighbours, so it is a source. A letter of $B_i$ with $i\ge2$ is not adjacent to the letters of $B_{i-1}$, which precede it, so it is not a source. Hence $B_1=F_1$, and inductively $B_i=F_i$, so $O$ is layered.

(c) Suppose $x\in F_i$ and $y\in F_{i+1}$ are adjacent in $\Gamma$. As $y\notin F_i$, it has an in-neighbour $x'$ outside $F_1\cup\cdots\cup F_{i-1}$. As $y$ is a source after removing $F_1\cup\cdots\cup F_i$, also $x'\in F_i$. Now $x'y$ is an edge of $\bar\Delta$ and $xy$ is not, so $x\ne x'$. Also $x$ and $x'$ are sources of the same orientation, so they are adjacent in $\Gamma$. Thus $x',x,y$ is an induced $3$-vertex path of $\Delta$. Since $F_1=\{z\}$ has one element and $x\ne x'$ lie in $F_i$, $i\ge2$, so the path avoids $z$, a contradiction.

(d) The only edge of $\bar\Delta$ at $z$ is $zu$, oriented $z\to u$. Every vertex other than $z$ and $u$ has an in-neighbour other than $z$, so the restriction $O'$ of $O$ to $S\setminus\{z\}$ has $u$ as its only source, and the layers of $O$ are $\{z\}$ followed by the layers of $O'$. The vertices $z$ and $u$ are not adjacent, and $O'$ is layered by (c) applied to $\Delta-z$ and $u$. $\square$

**Proof of part 2.** Put $g'=\tau_S(g)$. As in the proof of part 3 of the induced-subgraph theorem, $g'\in(G_S)_{(d)}\setminus(G_S)_{(d+1)}$, $e'_{S'}(g')\in(G_S)_{(d+1)}$ for every $S'\subsetneq S$, and it suffices to find a continuous $\rho:G_S\to U_{d+1}(\mathbb F_p)$ with $\rho(g')\ne1$, since then $\rho\tau_S$ does not kill $g$. By Lemma 3 there, applied to $\Gamma[S]$, $\mathrm{in}_d(g')=\mu_S(P)$ with $P\in\mathrm{Lie}_S\subseteq\mathcal L\cap T_\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the indicator of $S$. It is nonzero by Jennings' theorem. Let $z$ be a vertex lying on every induced $3$-vertex path of $\Gamma[S]$ in the first case, and the given $z$ in the second. By Lemma C(d) applied to $\Gamma[S]$, $c_t(P)\ne0$ for some $z$-initial class $t$. By Lemma D(a) its orientation has $z$ as its only source, so it is layered by Lemma D(c) or (d), and $t$ contains a block word $w$ by Lemma D(b). By Lemma B(ii), $\kappa_{\rho_w}(\mu_S(P))=c_t(P)\ne0$, and formula (1) gives $\rho_w(g')=1+c_t(P)E_{1,d+1}\ne1$. $\square$

**Proof of part 1.** It suffices to work over $\mathbb F_p$. Let $g\in G\setminus G_{(6)}$ and let $d$ be largest with $g\in G_{(d)}$. If $d\le4$, part 3 of the induced-subgraph theorem gives a continuous $\rho:G\to U_5(\mathbb F_p)$ with $\rho(g)\ne1$, and $M\mapsto\mathrm{diag}(M,1)$ embeds $U_5(\mathbb F_p)$ in $U_6(\mathbb F_p)$. So let $d=5$. As in the proof of part 3 there, choose $S$ of least size with $e_S(g)\notin G_{(6)}$, so that $|S|\le5$, and put $g'=\tau_S(g)$ and $\Delta=\Gamma[S]$. Then $g'\in(G_S)_{(5)}\setminus(G_S)_{(6)}$, $e'_{S'}(g')\in(G_S)_{(6)}$ for $S'\subsetneq S$, and it suffices to find a continuous $\rho:G_S\to U_6(\mathbb F_p)$ with $\rho(g')\ne1$.

*Case $|S|\le3$.* This is as in the proof of part 3 there: $\Delta$ is complete multipartite, or $G_S=\mathbb Z_p^2*\mathbb Z_p$, which has the property for $n\le2p+2$, and $6\le2p+2$.

*Case $|S|=4$.* By Lemma A(b), $\mathrm{in}_5(g')=\mu_S(P)$ with $P\in W\cap T_5$. The Möbius argument in the proof of Lemma 3 there, which does not use multilinearity, gives $\mathrm{in}_5(g')=\sum_\alpha\mu_S(P_\alpha)$, the sum over the multidegrees $\alpha$ of degree $5$ with support $S$. Each such $\alpha$ has one entry $2$ and three entries $1$, so by Lemma A(c) $P_\alpha$ is a combination of brackets of multidegree $\alpha$. If the complement of $\Delta$ is disconnected, $S=S_1\sqcup S_2$ as in Lemma 4(c). Its proof shows more generally that $\mu_S(m)=0$ for every bracket $m$ whose letters include vertices of both $S_1$ and $S_2$: write $m=[m_1,m_2]$; if the letters of $m_1$ or of $m_2$ meet both parts, induction applies, and otherwise $\mu_S(m)$ lies in $[\mathfrak g_1,\mathfrak g_2]$ or $[\mathfrak g_2,\mathfrak g_1]$, with $\mathfrak g_l$ as in that proof, and these are $0$. So $\mathrm{in}_5(g')=0$, contradicting Jennings' theorem. Otherwise $\Delta$ is one of the six graphs listed in the proof of part 3 there. If its components are complete, $G_S$ is a free pro-$p$ product of free abelian groups and has the property for $n\le2p+2$, as in that proof. There remain the path $a,b,c,d$ and the path $a,b,c$ with an isolated vertex $z$.

Suppose that $\rho_w(g')=1$ for every chain word $w$ of degree $5$ with support $S$. By Lemma B(i) and formula (1), $\rho_w(g')=1+\langle P_{\alpha(w)},X_w\rangle E_{1,6}$, where $\alpha(w)$ is the multidegree of $w$, so $\langle P_\alpha,X_w\rangle=0$ for every chain word $w$ of multidegree $\alpha$. We show $\mu_S(P_\alpha)=0$ for every $\alpha$, a contradiction. Let $r$ be the vertex with $\alpha_r=2$. We apply Lemma C to $\Gamma[S]$ with an anchor $z_0\ne r$, so that $\alpha_{z_0}=1$, such that every $z_0$-initial class is the class of a chain word. Such a class has one element, because no exchange applies to a chain word, so its coefficient $c_t(P_\alpha)=\langle P_\alpha,X_w\rangle$ vanishes, and Lemma C(d) gives $\mu_S(P_\alpha)=0$. By Lemma C(c), the $z_0$-initial classes are the classes of the words $z_0v$ such that no word equivalent to $v$ begins with a neighbour of $z_0$.

- *Path $a,b,c,d$, $r=a$.* Take $z_0=b$, whose neighbours are $a$ and $c$. The words $v$ have letters $a,a,c,d$, and $v$ must begin with $d$. Of $daac$, $daca$ and $dcaa$, the last is equivalent to $cdaa$. The words $bdaac$ and $bdaca$ are chain words, so no exchange applies to them or to $daac$ and $daca$, and their classes are the $b$-initial classes.
- *Path $a,b,c,d$, $r=b$.* Take $z_0=c$, with neighbours $b$ and $d$. The words $v$ have letters $a,b,b,d$ and must begin with $a$. Here $abbd\sim babd$ and $abdb\sim badb$, while $cadbb$ is a chain word, so its class is the only $c$-initial class.
- The cases $r=d$ and $r=c$ follow by exchanging $a$ with $d$ and $b$ with $c$.
- *Path $a,b,c$ and $z$, $r=a$.* Take $z_0=b$. Among $a,c,z$ there are no edges, so every class of such words has one element, and $v$ must begin with $z$. The words $bzaac$, $bzaca$ and $bzcaa$ are chain words. The case $r=c$ is symmetric.
- *Path $a,b,c$ and $z$, $r=z$.* Take $z_0=b$. Again $v$ must begin with $z$, which leaves the six words $z$ followed by an arrangement of $a,c,z$. The words $bv$ are chain words.

For $r=b$ take the anchor $z$, which has no neighbours, so the class of every $zv$ is $z$-initial. Here $v$ has letters $a,b,b,c$. By the pair invariant the order of $a$ and $c$ is constant on classes, and moving both letters $b$ to the end shows that every such word is equivalent to $acbb$ or to $cabb$. So there are two $z$-initial classes, $t_{ac}$ and $t_{ca}$, according to whether $a$ precedes $c$. The words $bbzac$ and $aczbb$ are chain words. By Lemma C(b) with $k=3$, $\langle\ell_v,X_{bbzac}\rangle$ is $1$ if $a$ precedes $c$ in $v$ and $0$ otherwise: $B$ must be the two positions of $b$. Similarly $\langle\ell_v,X_{aczbb}\rangle$ is $1$ if $c$ precedes $a$ and $0$ otherwise. By Lemma C(a), $\langle P_\alpha,X_{bbzac}\rangle=c_{t_{ac}}(P_\alpha)$ and $\langle P_\alpha,X_{aczbb}\rangle=c_{t_{ca}}(P_\alpha)$. Both vanish, so $\mu_S(P_\alpha)=0$ by Lemma C(d).

*Case $|S|=5$.* Then $d=|S|$ and, by Lemma 3 there, $\mathrm{in}_5(g')=\mu_S(P)\ne0$ with $P\in\mathrm{Lie}_S$. By Lemma 4(c) the complement of $\Delta$ is connected, so no vertex is adjacent to the four others. An induced $3$-vertex path is given by a non-adjacent pair, its ends, and a common neighbour of both. We show that $\Delta$ satisfies the hypothesis of part 2, unless $\Delta$ is a $5$-cycle or a $4$-cycle with an isolated vertex. Part 2 then applies to $g$, $d=5$ and $S$.

- *Some vertex $z$ has exactly three neighbours.* Let $u$ be its non-neighbour. If $u$ lies on every induced $3$-vertex path of $\Delta-z$, the second condition of part 2 holds. Otherwise $\Delta-z$ has an induced path $x,y,x'$ avoiding $u$. The complement of $\Delta-z$ is connected, because $z$ meets only $u$ in the complement of $\Delta$. So $y$ is not adjacent to $u$, as it would then be adjacent to all other vertices of $\Delta-z$. Also $u$ is not adjacent to both $x$ and $x'$, as $\Delta-z$ would then be the $4$-cycle $x,y,x',u$, whose complement is disconnected. After exchanging $x$ and $x'$, the edges of $\Delta$ are $xy,yx',zx,zy,zx'$, together with $ux$ or not. The non-adjacent pairs are $\{x,x'\}$ and the pairs containing $u$, except $\{u,x\}$ when $ux$ is an edge, and the induced $3$-vertex paths are $x,y,x'$ and $x,z,x'$, together with $u,x,y$ and $u,x,z$ when $u$ is adjacent to $x$. All contain $x$, so the first condition of part 2 holds.
- *Every vertex has at most two neighbours.* Then $\Delta$ is a disjoint union of paths and cycles. If it has no induced $3$-vertex path, the first condition holds vacuously. Otherwise some component is a path on at least three vertices or a cycle on at least four, and the remaining components have at most two vertices in all. So $\Delta$ is a $3$-vertex path with two further vertices, whose only induced $3$-vertex path is that path; a $4$-vertex path with an isolated vertex, whose two induced $3$-vertex paths share the inner vertices; a $5$-vertex path, whose three induced $3$-vertex paths contain the middle vertex; a $4$-cycle with an isolated vertex; or a $5$-cycle.

In the two remaining cases we use Lemma C with $\alpha$ the indicator of $S$, and the chain representations of Lemma 5, for which $\kappa_{\rho_w}(\mu_S(P))=\langle P,X_w\rangle$. Suppose that $\rho_w(g')=1$, so that $\langle P,X_w\rangle=0$, for each chain word $w$ below.

- *A $4$-cycle $a,b,a',b'$ and an isolated vertex $z$.* Take the anchor $z$. It has no neighbours, so the class of every $zv$ is $z$-initial. By Lemma D(a), the class of $v$ is determined by the order of $a,a'$ and the order of $b,b'$, since the complement of the $4$-cycle has the two edges $aa'$ and $bb'$. For $x\in\{a,a'\}$ and $y\in\{b,b'\}$, let $\bar x,\bar y$ be the other two vertices and $t_{x,y}$ the class of the words $zv$ with $x$ before $\bar x$ and $y$ before $\bar y$ in $v$. The word $w_{x,y}=\bar x\,x\,z\,y\,\bar y$ is a chain word. By Lemma C(b) with $k=3$, $\langle\ell_v,X_{w_{x,y}}\rangle$ is $1$ if $zv\in t_{x,y}$ and $0$ otherwise. By Lemma C(a), $\langle P,X_{w_{x,y}}\rangle=c_{t_{x,y}}(P)$, so all four coefficients vanish, and $\mu_S(P)=0$ by Lemma C(d), a contradiction.
- *A $5$-cycle.* Its complement is also a $5$-cycle. Name the vertices so that the complement is the cycle $z,v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4$. Then the neighbours of $z$ in $\Delta$ are $v_2,v_3$, and the other edges of $\Delta$ are $v_1v_3$, $v_1v_4$ and $v_2v_4$. By Lemma D(a), the $z$-initial classes correspond to the acyclic orientations of the complement with $z\to v_1$, $z\to v_4$, and neither $v_2$ nor $v_3$ a source of the path $v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4$. Of the eight orientations of this path, $v_2$ is a source in two and $v_3$ in two other ones. This leaves four $z$-initial classes. Writing each as the set of its words $zv$, the words $v$ are, for $t_1$: $v_1v_2v_3v_4$; for $t_2$: $v_4v_3v_2v_1$; for $t_3$, from $v_1\to v_2\leftarrow v_3\leftarrow v_4$: $v_1v_4v_3v_2$, $v_4v_1v_3v_2$, $v_4v_3v_1v_2$; and for $t_4$, from $v_1\to v_2\to v_3\leftarrow v_4$: $v_1v_2v_4v_3$, $v_1v_4v_2v_3$, $v_4v_1v_2v_3$. Consecutive vertices of the complementary cycle are non-adjacent in $\Delta$, so
$$w_1=zv_1v_2v_3v_4,\quad w_2=zv_4v_3v_2v_1,\quad w_3=v_2v_3v_4zv_1,\quad w_4=v_3v_4zv_1v_2$$
are chain words. By Lemma C(b), $\langle\ell_v,X_{w_1}\rangle$ and $\langle\ell_v,X_{w_2}\rangle$ are $1$ for $v=v_1v_2v_3v_4$, respectively $v=v_4v_3v_2v_1$, and $0$ otherwise. Next, $\langle\ell_v,X_{w_3}\rangle$ is $-1$ if $v$ lists $v_4,v_3,v_2$ in this order with $v_1$ inserted anywhere, and $0$ otherwise. These four words are those of $t_3$ and of $t_2$. Finally, $\langle\ell_v,X_{w_4}\rangle$ is $1$ if $v$ interleaves $v_4,v_3$ with $v_1,v_2$, and $0$ otherwise. These six words are those of $t_3$ and $t_4$. With $a_i=c_{t_i}(P)$, Lemma C(a) gives
$$\langle P,X_{w_1}\rangle=a_1,\quad\langle P,X_{w_2}\rangle=a_2,\quad\langle P,X_{w_3}\rangle=-a_2-a_3,\quad\langle P,X_{w_4}\rangle=a_3+a_4.$$
All four vanish, so $a_1=a_2=a_3=a_4=0$, and $\mu_S(P)=0$ by Lemma C(d), a contradiction.

In every case $g'$, and hence $g$, is detected. With the inclusion $\subseteq$, $G$ has the kernel $6$-unipotent property over $\mathbb F_p$, and hence over $\mathbb F$. $\square$

**Remarks.**

- *Degree seven.* By corollary (a), the case $n=7$ is decided by graphs on at most six vertices, through elements of degree $6$ whose least support $S$ has at most six vertices. Several inputs are then missing. For $|S|\le3$ at $p=2$, the group $\mathbb Z_2^2*\mathbb Z_2$ is outside the range $n\le2p+2$ of the polynomial-factor theorem. For $|S|=4$ and $|S|=5$ there are elements of degree $6$ that are not multilinear. For $|S|=6$, part 2 handles every graph to which its hypothesis applies, but not every graph. Let $\Delta$ be the graph whose complement is the tree obtained from a vertex by attaching one path of one edge and two paths of two edges. For each vertex $z$ there is exactly one acyclic orientation of this tree with $z$ as its only source, directing every edge away from $z$, and its layers are the distance classes from $z$. Such an orientation is layered only if every layer except the last is a single vertex, and that fails for every $z$. So by Lemma D(b) no $z$-initial class contains a block word, for any $z$, and the argument of part 2 does not apply to elements of degree $6$ whose least support is the vertex set of $\Delta$. Whether such elements are detected in $U_7(\mathbb F_p)$ is not decided here.
- *What part 2 uses.* Part 2 needs no restriction on $d$ or $p$. Lemma A is used only in part 1, for the elements of degree $5$ with four-vertex support, where one letter occurs twice.
