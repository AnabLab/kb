**Setting.** For a field $F$ of characteristic not $2$, let $W(F)$ be its Witt ring of nondegenerate symmetric bilinear forms, $k_*F = K^M_*(F)/2$ its mod-$2$ Milnor K-ring, $l(a)$ the class of $a \in F^\times$ in $k_1F = F^\times/F^{\times 2}$, and $\mathcal{G}_F$ its W-group, the quotient $G_F(2)/G_F(2)^4[G_F(2)^2, G_F(2)]$ of its maximal pro-$2$ Galois group (see [W-groups and Witt rings](w-groups-and-witt-rings.md)). The level $s(F)$ is the least $m$ such that $-1$ is a sum of $m$ squares in $F$, or $\infty$. Two fields are *Witt equivalent* if their Witt rings are isomorphic. Two global fields $F, E$ are *reciprocity equivalent* if there are a bijection $T$ from the primes of $F$, infinite ones included, to those of $E$ and a group isomorphism $t : F^\times/F^{\times 2} \to E^\times/E^{\times 2}$ with $(a, b)_P = (ta, tb)_{TP}$ for every prime $P$ of $F$ and all $a, b$, where $(\cdot,\cdot)_P$ is the Hilbert symbol [@szymiczek1991, §1, p. 316].

For a number field $K$, write $n = [K:\mathbb{Q}]$, $r$ for the number of real places, $s = s(K)$, $g$ for the number of dyadic primes $\mathfrak{p}_1, \dots, \mathfrak{p}_g$, and $n_i = [K_{\mathfrak{p}_i} : \mathbb{Q}_2]$ and $s_i = s(K_{\mathfrak{p}_i})$.

**Statement.**

1. *Local–global form.* Global fields of characteristic not $2$ are Witt equivalent exactly when they are reciprocity equivalent [@szymiczek1991, Theorem (1.1)]. Szymiczek proves this with arguments due to Perlis in his §§5–6 [@szymiczek1991, p. 316]. Witt equivalent number fields have the same degree [@szymiczek1991, §7, p. 328].
2. *Invariants.* The Witt equivalence class of a number field "is completely determined by" the invariants $(n, r, s, g; (n_i, s_i), i = 1, \dots, g)$ [@szymiczek1998, Remark 2, p. 59], where the dyadic primes are listed in no particular order. Szymiczek attributes this to Carpenter (Math. Z. 209 (1992)) and to Perlis, Szymiczek, Conner and Litherland (Contemp. Math. 155 (1994)), which have not been opened here. For each $n$ there are only finitely many Witt classes of fields of degree $n$, which Szymiczek credits to Carpenter. There are exactly $7$ classes of quadratic fields, represented by $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ with $d = -1, \pm 2, \pm 7, \pm 17$, a result Szymiczek credits to Czogała, and exactly $8$ classes of cubic fields [@szymiczek1991, §7, p. 329].
3. *Galois groups.* For number fields $K$ and $L$ the following are equivalent:
   - (a) $\mathcal{G}_K \cong \mathcal{G}_L$;
   - (b) $k_*K \cong k_*L$ as graded rings;
   - (c) $H^*(G_K, \mathbb{Z}/2) \cong H^*(G_L, \mathbb{Z}/2)$ as graded rings;
   - (d) $K$ and $L$ are Witt equivalent.

   So by part 2, the W-group of a number field, a quotient of $G_K$ of exponent $4$ and nilpotency class at most $2$, determines the invariants $(n, r, s, g; (n_i, s_i))$ and nothing more. This part is proved here.

**Examples.**

- $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{10})$, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{82})$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{130})$ lie in one Witt class, and $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{17})$, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{65})$, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{145})$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{1105})$ in another [@szymiczek1998, Table 1, p. 62]. By part 3, the fields in each list have isomorphic W-groups and isomorphic mod-$2$ cohomology rings, though they are pairwise non-isomorphic.
- Among quadratic fields, the class of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-1})$ contains only that field [@szymiczek1998, Introduction, p. 53], and by part 1 no field of another degree is Witt equivalent to it. Also $\mathbb{Q}$ "has a unique Witt ring in the class of all algebraic number fields" [@szymiczek1991, §7, p. 329]. So among number fields, the W-group determines $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-1})$ up to isomorphism.
- Every Witt class of number fields of even degree, other than the class of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-1})$, contains fields whose class groups have arbitrarily large $2$-rank [@szymiczek1998, Corollary, p. 62]. So, apart from the W-group of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-1})$, whose class group is trivial, every W-group of a number field of even degree occurs for fields with arbitrarily large class-group $2$-rank, and gives no bound on it.

**Proof of part 3.** The proof uses these standard facts, which have not been checked in a source here. For a nonarchimedean prime $P$ of $K$, the Hilbert symbol $(a, b)_P \in \{\pm 1\}$ of $a, b \in K_P^\times$ is $1$ exactly when $z^2 = ax^2 + by^2$ has a solution $(x, y, z) \ne 0$ in $K_P$. It is bimultiplicative, and $(a, 1 - a)_P = 1$ for $a \ne 0, 1$. If $P \nmid 2$, $u$ is a $P$-adic unit and $v_P(a) = 1$, then $(a, u)_P = 1$ exactly when $u$ is a square modulo $P$. Finally, by the Chebotarev density theorem, for $c \in K^\times \setminus K^{\times 2}$ infinitely many primes of $K$ are inert in $K(\sqrt{c})$, and for such a prime $P \nmid 2c$, $c$ is not a square modulo $P$.

By the universal property of $K^M_2(K)$, bimultiplicativity and the relation $(a, 1 - a)_P = 1$ give a homomorphism $K^M_2(K) \to \{\pm 1\}$, $\{a, b\} \mapsto (a, b)_P$, for $a, b \in K^\times$. As $\{\pm 1\}$ has exponent $2$, it factors through a homomorphism $h_P : k_2K \to \{\pm 1\}$ with $h_P(l(a)l(b)) = (a, b)_P$.

*Lemma 1.* If $c \in K^\times$ and $l(a)l(c) = 0$ in $k_2K$ for every $a \in K^\times$, then $c \in K^{\times 2}$.

*Proof.* Suppose $c \notin K^{\times 2}$. Choose a prime $P \nmid 2c$ inert in $K(\sqrt{c})$, so that $c$ is a unit at $P$ and not a square modulo $P$, and choose $a \in P \setminus P^2$. Then $h_P(l(a)l(c)) = (a, c)_P = -1$, so $l(a)l(c) \ne 0$. $\square$

*Lemma 2.* The form $\langle 1, 1 \rangle$ over $K$ is universal, that is, every element of $K^\times$ is a sum of two squares, exactly when $\sqrt{-1} \in K$.

*Proof.* If $i = \sqrt{-1} \in K$, then $x = \left(\frac{x+1}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{i(x-1)}{2}\right)^2$ for every $x$. If $\sqrt{-1} \notin K$, choose as in Lemma 1 a prime $P \nmid 2$ with $-1$ not a square modulo $P$, and $a \in P \setminus P^2$. If $a = s^2 + t^2$ with $s, t \in K$, then $(x, y, z) = (1, t, s)$ is a nonzero solution of $z^2 = a x^2 - y^2$, since $s^2 = a - t^2$, so $(a, -1)_P = 1$. But $(a, -1)_P = -1$, so $a$ is not a sum of two squares. $\square$

*The class of $-1$ is intrinsic.* In $K^M_2(K)$ we have $\{a, -a\} = 0$: for $a \ne 1$, write $-a = (1 - a)/(1 - a^{-1})$, so that $\{a, -a\} = \{a, 1 - a\} + \{a^{-1}, 1 - a^{-1}\} = 0$, and $\{1, -1\} = 0$. Hence $\{a, a\} = \{a, -1\}$, and $x^2 = x\, l(-1)$ for every $x \in k_1K$. If $e \in k_1K$ also satisfies $x^2 = xe$ for every $x \in k_1K$, then $x(e + l(-1)) = 0$ for every $x$, so $e = l(-1)$ by Lemma 1. So $l(-1)$ is the unique $e \in k_1K$ with $x^2 = xe$ for all $x \in k_1K$. Let $\varphi : k_*K \to k_*L$ be an isomorphism of graded rings. It restricts to a bijection $k_1K \to k_1L$ and respects products, so $\varphi(l(-1))$ has the same property in $k_*L$, and $\varphi(l(-1)) = l(-1)$. In particular $\sqrt{-1} \in K$ exactly when $\sqrt{-1} \in L$, since this says $l(-1) = 0$.

*The equivalences.* (b) and (c) are equivalent because, as $\mu_2 = \{\pm 1\}$ lies in every field, the norm residue isomorphism gives isomorphisms of graded rings $k_*K \cong H^*(G_K, \mathbb{Z}/2)$, by part 1 of "Consequences for cohomology rings" in [the norm residue isomorphism theorem](norm-residue-isomorphism-theorem.md). Adem, Karagueuzian and Mináč prove that $\mathcal{G}_F \cong \mathcal{G}_L$ exactly when $k_*F \cong k_*L$, that $W(F) \cong W(L)$ implies $k_*F \cong k_*L$, and that "the converse holds provided $F$ and $L$ have the same level if $\langle 1,1\rangle_F$ is universal" [@ademkaragueuzianminac1999, Corollary 3.9]. This gives (a)$\Leftrightarrow$(b) and (d)$\Rightarrow$(b). For (b)$\Rightarrow$(d), suppose $k_*K \cong k_*L$ and that $\langle 1, 1 \rangle$ is universal over $K$. By Lemma 2, $\sqrt{-1} \in K$, so $\sqrt{-1} \in L$ by the previous paragraph, and $s(K) = s(L) = 1$. So the level condition holds, and $W(K) \cong W(L)$. $\square$

**The class-2 quotient.** This is proved here. For a profinite group $H$, let $H(2)$ be its maximal pro-$2$ quotient and $\mathcal{W}(H) = H(2)/H(2)^4[H(2)^2, H(2)]$, so that $\mathcal{W}(G_K) = \mathcal{G}_K$. Let $G_K^{\mathrm{nil},2}$ be the maximal quotient of $G_K$ of nilpotency class at most $2$. Its maximal pro-$2$ quotient is $G/\overline{\gamma_3(G)}$ with $G = G_K(2)$, since both are the inverse limit of the finite quotients of $G_K$ that are $2$-groups of class at most $2$. Put $N = G^4[G^2, G]$. Commutators lie in $G^2$, as in the comparison in [W-groups and Witt rings](w-groups-and-witt-rings.md), so $\gamma_3(G) = [[G, G], G] \subseteq [G^2, G] \subseteq N$, and $N$ is closed. Hence $\mathcal{W}(G_K^{\mathrm{nil},2}) = G/\overline{\gamma_3(G)}N = G/N = \mathcal{G}_K$. So an isomorphism of the class-$2$ quotients gives one of the W-groups, and by part 3 the fields are Witt equivalent. In particular, the fields $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})$ with $d = 11, 19, 43, 67, 163$, whose class-$2$ quotients are isomorphic by [Koymans and Pagano](two-step-nilpotent-not-anabelian.md), are pairwise Witt equivalent. Consistently with part 2, each has $n = 2$, $r = 0$, $g = 1$ and $n_1 = 2$, since $-d \equiv 5 \pmod 8$, so that $2$ is inert.

Isomorphic abelianizations do not give isomorphic W-groups. By [@angelakisstevenhagen2013, Theorem 4.1], $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})$ have isomorphic $G_K^{\mathrm{ab}}$. But $-3 \equiv 5$ and $-7 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$, so $2$ is inert in the first field and splits in the second, giving $g = 1$ and $g = 2$. As $g$ is one of the invariants of the Witt class in part 2, the two fields are not Witt equivalent, and their W-groups are not isomorphic, by part 3.

**Role in the graph.** The full group $G_K$ determines a number field, by the [Neukirch–Uchida theorem](neukirch-uchida-theorem.md), but its W-group, and with it the whole mod-$2$ cohomology ring, retains only the data of part 2. By parts 1 and 3, an isomorphism of W-groups amounts to one isomorphism of square class groups matching the Hilbert symbols at all places, so it is a local–global condition. The W-group still determines the degree, which its Frattini quotient $\mathcal{G}_K/\Phi(\mathcal{G}_K)$ does not: that quotient is the Pontryagin dual of $K^\times/K^{\times 2}$, an $\mathbb{F}_2$-vector space of countably infinite dimension for every number field $K$.
