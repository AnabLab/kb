**Terminology.** Here a *curve* over an algebraically closed field $k$ of characteristic $0$ is a smooth, proper, connected curve over $k$. For a finite morphism $f : X \to \mathbb{P}^1_k$, a *critical value* is a point of $\mathbb{P}^1(k)$ with fewer than $\deg f$ preimages [@koeck2004, §2, p. 7]. Since the ramification indices over a point add up to $\deg f$, the critical values are the points over which $f$ ramifies. Write $\mathrm{Crit}(f)$ for the set of them. A *Belyi map* is a dominant morphism $\phi : X \to \mathbb{P}^1$ that is unramified over $U_P = \mathbb{P}^1 \setminus \{0, 1, \infty\}$, and $\phi^{-1}(U_P)$ is then a *Belyi open* of $X$ [@mochizuki2004noncritical, Definition 1, p. 105]. Mochizuki states this over $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$, and the same words make sense over any $k$.

**Statement.**

1. *Belyi's theorem.* A curve $X$ over $\mathbb{C}$ is defined over a number field if and only if there is a finite morphism $X \to \mathbb{P}^1_{\mathbb{C}}$ with at most $3$ critical values [@koeck2004, Theorem 3.3, p. 10]. Köck attributes it to Belyi's 1979 paper [@koeck2004, Introduction, p. 1]. Composing with a fractional linear transformation moves three points to $0, 1, \infty$, so the condition is that $X$ has a Belyi map.
2. *Prescribed cusps, over the field of definition.* Let $X$ be a smooth, proper, geometrically connected curve over a number field $F \subset \bar{\mathbb{Q}}$, and let $\Sigma \subseteq X(\bar{\mathbb{Q}})$ be finite. Then there is a finite morphism $\beta : X \to \mathbb{P}^1_F$ such that $\beta_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}$ is a Belyi map and $\beta(\Sigma) \subseteq \{0, 1, \infty\}$. It is the case $T = \varnothing$ of part 3, and it is proved below by Köck's elementary proof of the existence direction of part 1, with $\Sigma$ added.
3. *Noncritical Belyi maps (Mochizuki).* Let $X$ be a curve over $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$, and let $S, T \subseteq X(\bar{\mathbb{Q}})$ be finite and disjoint. Then there is a Belyi map $\phi : X \to \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}$ with $\phi(S) \subseteq \{0, 1, \infty\}$ and $\phi(T) \cap \{0, 1, \infty\} = \varnothing$. If $X$, $S$ and $T$ are defined over a number field $F$, then $\phi$ may be taken to be defined over $F$ [@mochizuki2004noncritical, Theorem 2.5, p. 109]. In particular $\phi$ is unramified at every point of $T$.
4. *Belyi opens form a Zariski base.* If $V \subseteq X$ is open and contains a closed point $x$, then some Belyi open $U_X$ satisfies $x \in U_X \subseteq V$ [@mochizuki2004noncritical, Corollary 1.1, p. 105]. This is part 3 with $S = X \setminus V$ and $T = \{x\}$, a consequence that Mochizuki credits to Tamagawa [@mochizuki2004noncritical, §1, p. 105].

Mochizuki also gives finitely many Belyi maps that serve, in the sense of part 3, for every $T$ of a fixed cardinality disjoint from $S$ [@mochizuki2004noncritical, Corollary 3.1, p. 111], and a version in which, for every set of at most $c$ points of degree at most $d$ over $F$, one of finitely many Belyi maps sends all their conjugates into fixed compact subsets of $U_P(L_v)$, for finitely many places $v$ of $F$ and suitable completions $L_v$ of number fields containing $F_v$ [@mochizuki2004noncritical, Corollary 3.2, pp. 111–112].

**Consequences used in the knowledgebase.** These are checked here.

- *Belyi opens are finite étale over the tripod.* Let $\phi$ be a Belyi map. Then $\phi^{-1}(U_P) \to U_P$ is finite, as a base change of a finite morphism, and flat, because a dominant morphism of smooth curves is flat. It is unramified by assumption, so it is finite étale. Now let $U$ be obtained from a smooth, proper, geometrically connected curve $X$ over a number field $F$ by removing finitely many closed points. Part 2, with $\Sigma$ the set of geometric points of $X \setminus U$, gives a dense open subscheme $\beta^{-1}(\mathbb{P}^1_F \setminus \{0, 1, \infty\})$ of $U$, finite étale over $\mathbb{P}^1_F \setminus \{0, 1, \infty\}$. By part 3, applied with $T$ the set of geometric points over a given closed point of $U$, it can be chosen to contain that point.
- *Where this is used.* The existence direction of part 1 gives the [injectivity of $G_{\mathbb{Q}}$ on the geometric fundamental group of the tripod](injectivity-of-outer-galois-representations.md), and André uses it in describing [local Galois groups through tempered fundamental groups](local-galois-groups-via-tempered-fundamental-groups.md). The first consequence is the step through which [Tamagawa's criterion](lubin-tate-criterion-for-semi-absolute-anabelian-geometry.md) reaches every abelian variety with complex multiplication. Part 2 is the form used for [decomposition groups of curves of Belyi type](decomposition-groups-of-p-adic-curves-of-belyi-type.md). Parts 3 and 4 are the forms used for [birational sections with local conditions](birational-sections-with-local-conditions.md) and in the [combinatorial construction of $G_{\mathbb{Q}}$](combinatorial-construction-of-the-absolute-galois-group-of-q.md).
- *Proper curves.* A Belyi open is never all of $X$, because it is finite over the affine curve $U_P$, hence affine, while $X$ is proper of dimension $1$. Relatedly, no finite étale cover of a proper curve maps dominantly to the tripod, which is why Tamagawa's criterion never applies to proper curves; see [the A-qLT problem](a-qlt-problem-for-p-adic-hyperbolic-curves.md), where this is proved.

**Variant with ramification indices $3$ and $2$.** This is proved here. Every curve $X$ over $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ has a finite morphism $X \to \mathbb{P}^1$, unramified over $U_P$, all of whose ramification indices over $0$ equal $3$ and all of whose ramification indices over $1$ equal $2$.

*Proof.* Let $\beta$ be a Belyi map of $X$, and put
$$J(\lambda) = \frac{4(\lambda^2 - \lambda + 1)^3}{27\lambda^2(\lambda - 1)^2}, \qquad J(\lambda) - 1 = \frac{\big((\lambda + 1)(\lambda - 2)(2\lambda - 1)\big)^2}{27\lambda^2(\lambda - 1)^2}.$$
The identity is checked by comparing the two polynomials of degree $6$, $4(\lambda^2 - \lambda + 1)^3 - 27\lambda^2(\lambda - 1)^2$ and $\big((\lambda+1)(\lambda-2)(2\lambda-1)\big)^2$, at seven rational values of $\lambda$. The numerator of $J$ has degree $6$ and no root at $0$ or $1$, so $J$ has degree $6$. It has index $2$ at each of $\lambda = 0, 1, \infty$ over $\infty$, index $3$ at each root of $\lambda^2 - \lambda + 1$ over $0$, and index $2$ at each of $\lambda = -1, 2, 1/2$ over $1$. These contribute $3 + 2 \cdot 2 + 3 = 10 = 2 \cdot 6 - 2$ to the Riemann–Hurwitz formula for $\mathbb{P}^1 \to \mathbb{P}^1$, so $J$ has no other ramification, and $\mathrm{Crit}(J) = \{0, 1, \infty\}$ with $J(\{0, 1, \infty\}) = \{\infty\}$. By the composition rule in the proof below, $J \circ \beta$ is unramified over $U_P$. If $J(\beta(x)) = 0$, then $\beta(x)$ is a root of $\lambda^2 - \lambda + 1$, which lies in $U_P$, so $\beta$ is unramified at $x$ and $e_x(J \circ \beta) = e_{\beta(x)}(J) = 3$. In the same way every point over $1$ has index $2$. $\square$

In the language of uniformization, this is the statement, usually credited to Belyi, that every curve over $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ minus finitely many points is the quotient of the upper half plane by a finite-index subgroup of $\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$. That reformulation, and the identification of $1728J$ with the $j$-invariant of the Legendre curve $y^2 = x(x-1)(x-\lambda)$, are not checked in a source here.

**Proof of part 2.** This follows Köck's proof of the existence direction of Belyi's theorem [@koeck2004, proof of Theorem 3.3, and Lemmas 3.5 and 3.6, pp. 10–11], with the set $\Sigma$ carried along. All curves and morphisms are taken over $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ unless a smaller field is named.

*Composition rule.* For finite morphisms $f : X \to Y$ and $g : Y \to Z$ of curves, $\mathrm{Crit}(g \circ f) = \mathrm{Crit}(g) \cup g(\mathrm{Crit}(f))$ [@koeck2004, proof of Theorem 3.3, p. 10]. Indeed $e_x(g \circ f) = e_{f(x)}(g)\,e_x(f)$, so $g \circ f$ ramifies at $x$ exactly when $g$ ramifies at $f(x)$ or $f$ ramifies at $x$. Every point of $Y$ has a preimage under $f$, so the points of $Z$ over which $g \circ f$ ramifies are those in $\mathrm{Crit}(g)$ or in $g(\mathrm{Crit}(f))$.

*Step 1: moving algebraic points to rational ones* [@koeck2004, Lemma 3.5, p. 11]. For every finite $S \subseteq \mathbb{P}^1(\bar{\mathbb{Q}})$ there is a nonconstant $p \in \mathbb{Q}[z]$ with $p(S) \cup \mathrm{Crit}(p) \subseteq \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})$. A polynomial fixes $\infty$, so only $S' = S \setminus \{\infty\}$ matters, and one may enlarge $S'$ to be stable under $\mathrm{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})$. Induct on $n = |S'|$. If $S' \subseteq \mathbb{Q}$, which holds when $n \le 1$, take $p = z$. Otherwise $n \ge 2$. Let $p_1 \in \mathbb{Q}[z]$ be the product of the distinct minimal polynomials over $\mathbb{Q}$ of the elements of $S'$. It has degree $n$ and vanishes on $S'$. A polynomial of degree at least $2$ ramifies at $\infty$, and at a finite point $r$ exactly when $p_1'(r) = 0$. So $\mathrm{Crit}(p_1) = S_1 \cup \{\infty\}$, where $S_1 = p_1(\{r : p_1'(r) = 0\})$. The set $S_1$ is stable under $\mathrm{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})$, since $p_1$ and $p_1'$ have rational coefficients, and $|S_1| \le n - 1$. By induction there is $p_2 \in \mathbb{Q}[z]$ with $p_2(S_1) \cup \mathrm{Crit}(p_2) \subseteq \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})$. Then $p = p_2 \circ p_1$ satisfies $p(S') = \{p_2(0)\} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}$ and, by the composition rule, $\mathrm{Crit}(p) = \mathrm{Crit}(p_2) \cup p_2(S_1 \cup \{\infty\}) \subseteq \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})$.

*Step 2: moving rational points to $0, 1, \infty$* [@koeck2004, Lemma 3.6, p. 11]. For every finite $T \subseteq \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})$ there is a nonconstant $q \in \mathbb{Q}(z)$ with $q(T) \cup \mathrm{Crit}(q) \subseteq \{0, 1, \infty\}$. Induct on $r = |T|$. If $r \le 3$, a fractional linear transformation over $\mathbb{Q}$ maps $T$ into $\{0, 1, \infty\}$, and it has no critical values. Let $r \ge 4$. A fractional linear transformation $\mu$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ sends three points of $T$ to $0, 1, \infty$ and a fourth to some $c \in \mathbb{Q} \setminus \{0, 1\}$. The transformations $z \mapsto 1/z$ and $z \mapsto z/(z-1)$ permute $\{0, 1, \infty\}$, and they send $c$ into the interval $(0, 1)$ when $c > 1$ and when $c < 0$, respectively. So one may assume that $0, 1, \infty \in T$ and that $m/(m+n) \in T$ for some integers $m, n \ge 1$. Put
$$q_1(z) = \frac{(m+n)^{m+n}}{m^m n^n}\, z^m (1 - z)^n.$$
Then $q_1(0) = q_1(1) = 0$, $q_1(\infty) = \infty$ and $q_1(m/(m+n)) = 1$. Since $q_1'(z)$ is a constant multiple of $z^{m-1}(1-z)^{n-1}\big(m - (m+n)z\big)$, the only possible ramification points are $0$, $1$, $m/(m+n)$ and $\infty$, so $\mathrm{Crit}(q_1) \subseteq \{0, 1, \infty\}$. The set $T_1 = q_1(T) \subseteq \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})$ has at most $r - 1$ elements and contains $0, 1, \infty$. By induction there is $q_2$ with $q_2(T_1) \cup \mathrm{Crit}(q_2) \subseteq \{0, 1, \infty\}$, so $q_2(\{0, 1, \infty\}) \subseteq \{0, 1, \infty\}$. Then $q = q_2 \circ q_1$ satisfies $q(T) = q_2(T_1) \subseteq \{0, 1, \infty\}$ and $\mathrm{Crit}(q) = \mathrm{Crit}(q_2) \cup q_2(\mathrm{Crit}(q_1)) \subseteq \{0, 1, \infty\}$.

*Step 3: assembly.* The function field of $X$ over $F$ has transcendence degree $1$, so it contains a nonconstant $t'$, which defines a finite morphism $t' : X \to \mathbb{P}^1_F$. Put $S_1 = \mathrm{Crit}(t'_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}) \cup t'(\Sigma)$, a finite subset of $\mathbb{P}^1(\bar{\mathbb{Q}})$, and take $p$ as in Step 1 for $S_1$. Put $T = \mathrm{Crit}(p) \cup p(S_1) \subseteq \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})$, and take $q$ as in Step 2 for $T$. Let $\beta = q \circ p \circ t'$, which is defined over $F$ because $p$ and $q$ have rational coefficients. By the composition rule, $\mathrm{Crit}(p \circ t') = \mathrm{Crit}(p) \cup p(\mathrm{Crit}(t')) \subseteq T$, so
$$\mathrm{Crit}(\beta) = \mathrm{Crit}(q) \cup q(\mathrm{Crit}(p \circ t')) \subseteq \{0, 1, \infty\} \cup q(T) \subseteq \{0, 1, \infty\},$$
and $\beta(\Sigma) = q(p(t'(\Sigma))) \subseteq q(p(S_1)) \subseteq q(T) \subseteq \{0, 1, \infty\}$. $\square$

**The other direction of part 1.** Köck proves that $X$ is defined over a number field when it has a finite morphism $t$ to $\mathbb{P}^1_{\mathbb{C}}$ with critical values in $\{0, 1, \infty\}$, in three steps [@koeck2004, proof of Theorem 3.3, p. 10].

- There are only finitely many isomorphism classes of pairs $(X, t)$ of a given degree with critical values in a given finite set, because they correspond to subgroups of given index in the finitely generated fundamental group of the punctured sphere [@koeck2004, Proposition 3.1, pp. 9–10].
- Hence the field fixed by the automorphisms $\sigma$ of $\mathbb{C}$ for which $(X^\sigma, t^\sigma) \cong (X, t)$, the moduli field of $t$, is a number field [@koeck2004, Corollary 3.2, p. 10].
- $X$ and $t$ are defined over a finite extension of the moduli field of $t$ [@koeck2004, Theorem 2.2, p. 7]. The proof uses Riemann–Roch to write the function field of $X$ as generated over $\mathbb{C}(t)$ by one normalized function, whose minimal polynomial is invariant under a subgroup of finite index [@koeck2004, proof of Theorem 2.2, pp. 7–8].

Köck also deduces an explicit bound for the degree of a number field over which $X$ and $t$ are defined, in terms of $\deg t$ [@koeck2004, Corollary 3.7, p. 12].
