**Setting.** Fix a prime $p$ and a power $q=p^f$, $f\ge1$. Let
$$G=\langle x,y,z\mid[x,y]=1\rangle_{\text{pro-}p}=\mathbb Z_p^2*\mathbb Z_p,$$
with $p$-Zassenhaus filtration $G_{(n)}$. As in [the large-field theorem](kernel-unipotent-property-over-large-finite-fields-for-free-products.md), a pro-$p$ group $H$ has the *kernel $n$-unipotent property over $\mathbb F_q$* if $H_{(n)}=\bigcap_\rho\ker\rho$ over all continuous $\rho:H\to U_n(\mathbb F_q)$. For $q=p$ this is the kernel $n$-unipotent property defined in [the counterexample over $\mathbb F_p$](kernel-unipotent-conjecture-fails-for-zp2-free-product-zp.md), the equality of Mináč and Tân's [conjecture](kernel-unipotent-conjecture.md). As $U_n(\mathbb F_p)\subseteq U_n(\mathbb F_q)$, the property over $\mathbb F_p$ implies the property over $\mathbb F_q$, so failure over $\mathbb F_q$ is the stronger statement. Put
$$N(q)=(q+1)(q^2-q+3),\qquad n_0(q)=N(q)+3\ \text{if }p=2,\qquad n_0(q)=N(q)+4\ \text{if }p\text{ is odd}.$$
So $n_0(p)$ is the bound $n_0$ of the counterexample, and $n_0(4)=78$, $n_0(8)=534$ and $n_0(9)=754$.

**Theorem.** This is proved here. For every $n\ge n_0(q)$ there is $g\in G_{(n-1)}$ with $g\notin G_{(n)}$ and $\rho(g)=1$ for every continuous $\rho:G\to U_n(\mathbb F_q)$. So $G$ fails the kernel $n$-unipotent property over $\mathbb F_q$ for every $n\ge n_0(q)$.

**Corollary 1 (no fixed field suffices).** For every $q$, $G$ has the kernel $n$-unipotent property over $\mathbb F_q$ for every $n\le2q+2$, and fails it for every $n\ge n_0(q)$. So each fixed finite coefficient field detects the Zassenhaus filtration only up to a bounded matrix size, while each matrix size $n$ is detected over every $\mathbb F_q$ with $2q+2\ge n$. The same holds for $G_K(p)$, where $K$ is the field containing a primitive $p$th root of unity with $G_K(p)\cong G$ given by Corollary 1 of [the counterexample](kernel-unipotent-conjecture-fails-for-zp2-free-product-zp.md). So the kernel-unipotent conjecture for Galois groups stays false when $\mathbb F_p$ is replaced by any fixed finite field.

*Proof.* $G$ is the free pro-$p$ product of the free abelian group $\mathbb Z_p^2$ and the free group $\mathbb Z_p$, so the first assertion is [the polynomial factor theorem](polynomial-factors-double-the-unipotent-detection-range.md) with $\mathbb F=\mathbb F_q$ and $Q=q$. The second is the theorem. $\square$

**Corollary 2 (retracts and right-angled Artin groups).** This is proved here.

- (i) Let $G'$ be a pro-$p$ group with continuous homomorphisms $\sigma:G\to G'$ and $\tau:G'\to G$ such that $\tau\sigma=\mathrm{id}_G$. Then $G'$ fails the kernel $n$-unipotent property over $\mathbb F_q$ for every $n\ge n_0(q)$. This applies to the groups $(H_1\times H_2)*H_3$ of Corollary 2(ii) of [the counterexample](kernel-unipotent-conjecture-fails-for-zp2-free-product-zp.md), such as $\mathbb Z_p^2*F$ and $(\mathbb Z_p\times F)*\mathbb Z_p$ for a nontrivial free pro-$p$ group $F$ of finite rank, since that corollary constructs such $\sigma$ and $\tau$.
- (ii) Let $\Gamma$ be a finite nonempty simple graph, and $G_\Gamma$ its right-angled Artin pro-$p$ group, as in [the classification over $\mathbb F_p$](kernel-unipotent-property-for-right-angled-artin-pro-p-groups.md). If $\Gamma$ is complete multipartite, $G_\Gamma$ has the kernel $n$-unipotent property over every $\mathbb F_q$ for every $n$. Otherwise, for every $q$, it fails the property over $\mathbb F_q$ for every $n\ge n_0(q)$.

*Proof.* (i) Take $g$ as in the theorem. Continuous homomorphisms map each term of the $p$-Zassenhaus filtration into the corresponding term, so $\sigma(g)\in G'_{(n-1)}$, and $\sigma(g)\notin G'_{(n)}$, since otherwise $g=\tau\sigma(g)\in G_{(n)}$. For every continuous $\rho:G'\to U_n(\mathbb F_q)$, the composite $\rho\sigma$ is a continuous homomorphism $G\to U_n(\mathbb F_q)$, so $\rho(\sigma(g))=1$.

(ii) If $\Gamma$ is complete multipartite, $G_\Gamma$ has the property over $\mathbb F_p$ for every $n$, by the implication from item 2 to item 4 of the linked classification, hence over every $\mathbb F_q$. Otherwise, by the equivalence of items 1 and 2 there, $\Gamma$ has vertices $a,b,c$ with $a,b$ adjacent and $c$ adjacent to neither. A continuous homomorphism out of a right-angled Artin pro-$p$ group is a map on vertices sending adjacent vertices to commuting elements. So $\sigma:G\to G_\Gamma$, sending $x,y,z$ to $a,b,c$, is defined, as $a$ and $b$ commute. So is $\tau:G_\Gamma\to G$, sending $a,b,c$ to $x,y,z$ and every other vertex to $1$: the only edge among $a,b,c$ is $\{a,b\}$, which goes to the commuting pair $x,y$, and every other edge has an endpoint sent to $1$. Then $\tau\sigma$ fixes $x,y,z$, so $\tau\sigma=\mathrm{id}_G$, and (i) applies. $\square$

**Proof of the theorem.** Throughout, $k$ is a finite field of characteristic $p$. Steps 1–3 and Step 4(a) of the proof in [the counterexample](kernel-unipotent-conjecture-fails-for-zp2-free-product-zp.md) are stated and proved there for every such $k$, and are used here unchanged, with their notation: $m=n-1$; for a continuous $\rho:G\to U_{m+1}(k)$, the superdiagonal vectors $u_j=(\alpha_j,\beta_j,\gamma_j)$ and $\pi_j=(\alpha_j,\beta_j)$; the decoupled representations; the elements $w_\mu\in G$ and $\ell_\mu\in R=\mathbb F_p\langle X,Y,Z\rangle$; formula (1), $\rho(g)=1+\langle h,u\rangle E_{1,m+1}$; the maps $\kappa$ and $\kappa_{p_1,p_2}$; and formula (2). Step 4 there also gives
$$\langle h,u\rangle=\sum_{p_1<p_2}\gamma_{p_1}\gamma_{p_2}\,S_{p_1,p_2},\qquad S_{p_1,p_2}=\sum_Wh_W\prod_{j\ne p_1,p_2}\pi_j(W_j),$$
the inner sum running over the words $W$ with $Z$ at positions $p_1,p_2$. Only Step 4(b), Step 5 and the vanishing part of Step 6 used $k=\mathbb F_p$. They are replaced by Steps A to C below.

*Step A: runs over $\mathbb F_q$.* Let $\rho:G\to U_{m+1}(\mathbb F_q)$ be continuous and $p_1<p_2$. Then either $S_{p_1,p_2}=0$, or $S_{p_1,p_2}=\bigl(\prod_{j\ne p_1,p_2}\lambda_j\bigr)\kappa_{p_1,p_2}(h)(v_0,v_1,v_2)$ for some $\lambda_j\in\mathbb F_q$ and $v_0,v_1,v_2\in\mathbb F_q^2$.

Indeed, consecutive $\pi_j$ are linearly dependent, by Step 2 there. If $\pi_j=0$ for some $j\notin\{p_1,p_2\}$, every word in $S_{p_1,p_2}$ has $X$ or $Y$ at position $j$, so its term contains the factor $0$, and $S_{p_1,p_2}=0$. Otherwise, let $J_0=[1,p_1-1]$, $J_1=[p_1+1,p_2-1]$ and $J_2=[p_2+1,m]$. All $\pi_j$ with $j\in J_l$ are nonzero, and two consecutive ones are dependent, so they span the same line. Hence they all lie on one line $\mathbb F_qv_l$ with $v_l\in\mathbb F_q^2$, taking $v_l=0$ if $J_l$ is empty, and $\pi_j=\lambda_jv_l$. Step 4(a) there gives the formula.

*Step B: a polynomial over $\mathbb F_p$ vanishing on the $\mathbb F_q$-configurations.* Write $\delta(v,d)=v'd''-v''d'$ for $v=(v',v'')$ and $d=(d',d'')$. The vectors $r_t=(1,t)$ for $t\in\mathbb F_q$ and $r_\infty=(0,1)$ form a set $\mathcal R$ containing exactly one vector on each of the $q+1$ lines of $\mathbb F_q^2$: a nonzero $(a,b)$ is a multiple of $r_{b/a}$ if $a\ne0$, and of $r_\infty$ if $a=0$. For $r\in\mathcal R$, the linear form $\delta(r,\cdot)$ on $\mathbb F_q^2$ has kernel $\mathbb F_qr$, and every nonzero linear form on $\mathbb F_q^2$ is $c\,\delta(r,\cdot)$ for exactly one $r\in\mathcal R$ and one $c\in\mathbb F_q^\times$, as forms with the same kernel are proportional. In $\mathbb F_q[d_1,d_2]$, with $d_i=(d_i',d_i'')$, let $\Psi_q$ be the product of the following $3(q+1)+(q+1)q(q-1)=N(q)$ linear forms:

- $\delta(r,d_1)$, $\delta(r,d_2)$ and $\delta(r,d_1+d_2)$, for each $r\in\mathcal R$;
- $\delta(r,d_1)+c\,\delta(r',d_2)$, for each ordered pair $r\ne r'$ in $\mathcal R$ and each $c\in\mathbb F_q^\times$.

For $q=p$ this is the polynomial $\Psi$ of Step 5 there, for this choice of representatives.

(B1) *For all $v_0,v_1,v_2\in\mathbb F_q^2$, some factor $\ell$ of $\Psi_q$ vanishes at $(v_0,0)$, $(-v_1,v_1)$ and $(0,-v_2)$.* This is the claim of Step 5 there, whose proof uses only that the coefficients form a field. Here it is over $\mathbb F_q$. Write a linear form as $\ell(d_1,d_2)=\lambda_1(d_1)+\lambda_2(d_2)$. The conditions are $\lambda_1(v_0)=0$, $\lambda_1(v_1)=\lambda_2(v_1)$ and $\lambda_2(v_2)=0$, three linear conditions on the $4$-dimensional space of pairs $(\lambda_1,\lambda_2)$, so they have a nonzero solution. If $\lambda_2=0$, then $\lambda_1=c\,\delta(r,\cdot)$ and $\ell$ is a multiple of the factor $\delta(r,d_1)$; similarly if $\lambda_1=0$. Otherwise $\lambda_1=c\,\delta(r,\cdot)$ and $\lambda_2=c'\delta(r',\cdot)$. If $r\ne r'$, $\ell$ is $c$ times the factor $\delta(r,d_1)+(c'/c)\,\delta(r',d_2)$. If $r=r'$ and $\delta(r,v_1)\ne0$, the middle condition gives $c=c'$, and $\ell=c\,\delta(r,d_1+d_2)$. If $r=r'$ and $\delta(r,v_1)=0$, then $(\lambda_1,0)$ also satisfies all three conditions, and the factor $\delta(r,d_1)$ works.

(B2) *$\Psi_q$ has coefficients in $\mathbb F_p$.* Let $\varphi$ be the ring automorphism of $\mathbb F_q[d_1,d_2]$ that raises every coefficient to its $p$th power and fixes the variables. Then $\varphi(\delta(r,d))=\delta(\varphi(r),d)$, where $\varphi(r)$ raises both coordinates to the $p$th power. Now $\varphi(r_t)=r_{t^p}$ and $\varphi(r_\infty)=r_\infty$, and $t\mapsto t^p$ is a bijection of $\mathbb F_q$, so $\varphi$ permutes $\mathcal R$. Hence $\varphi$ sends $\delta(r,d_1)$, $\delta(r,d_2)$ and $\delta(r,d_1+d_2)$ to the same forms for $\varphi(r)$, and sends $\delta(r,d_1)+c\,\delta(r',d_2)$ to $\delta(\varphi(r),d_1)+c^p\,\delta(\varphi(r'),d_2)$, where $(r,r',c)\mapsto(\varphi(r),\varphi(r'),c^p)$ is a bijection of the index set of the second family. So $\varphi$ permutes the factors of $\Psi_q$, and $\varphi(\Psi_q)=\Psi_q$. Every coefficient of $\Psi_q$ is therefore fixed by $t\mapsto t^p$, so lies in $\mathbb F_p$.

As a check, the factors can be multiplied out by family. From $\prod_{t\in\mathbb F_q}(T-t)=T^q-T$ and $\prod_{c\in\mathbb F_q^\times}(T-c)=T^{q-1}-1$, homogenized, one gets $\prod_{r\in\mathcal R}\delta(r,d)=-d'\prod_t(d''-td')=d'^{\,q}d''-d'd''^{\,q}=:\Lambda(d)$ and $\prod_{c\in\mathbb F_q^\times}(A+cB)=A^{q-1}-B^{q-1}$. So
$$\Psi_q=\Lambda(d_1)\,\Lambda(d_2)\,\Lambda(d_1+d_2)\prod_{r\ne r'}\bigl(\delta(r,d_1)^{q-1}-\delta(r',d_2)^{q-1}\bigr),$$
and $\varphi$ visibly fixes $\Lambda$ and permutes the factors of the last product.

(B3) *$\Psi_q$ is symmetric.* Let $\sigma$ swap $d_1$ and $d_2$. It exchanges $\delta(r,d_1)$ and $\delta(r,d_2)$, fixes $\delta(r,d_1+d_2)$, and sends $\delta(r,d_1)+c\,\delta(r',d_2)$ to $c\,\bigl(\delta(r',d_1)+c^{-1}\delta(r,d_2)\bigr)$, where $(r,r',c)\mapsto(r',r,c^{-1})$ is a bijection of the index set. So $\Psi_q\circ\sigma=\epsilon\Psi_q$, where $\epsilon$ is the product, over the $q(q+1)$ ordered pairs, of $\prod_{c\in\mathbb F_q^\times}c=-1$. As $q(q+1)$ is even, $\epsilon=1$.

For $D\ge N(q)$ put $\Phi_D=\Psi_q\,(d_1'+d_2')^{D-N(q)}$ if $p=2$, and for $D\ge N(q)+1$ put $\Phi_D=\Psi_q\,(d_1'-d_2')(d_1'+d_2')^{D-N(q)-1}$ if $p$ is odd. Then $\Phi_D$ is a nonzero homogeneous polynomial of degree $D$ with coefficients in $\mathbb F_p$, symmetric under $\sigma$ if $p=2$ and antisymmetric if $p$ is odd. This is all that Step 6 there uses of $\Phi_D$ to build the element and to detect it.

*Step C: the element.* Let $n\ge n_0(q)$, $m=n-1$ and $D=m-2$, so $\Phi_D$ is defined. Write $\Phi_D=\sum c_{\mu\nu}\,d_1^\mu d_2^\nu$ with $c_{\mu\nu}\in\mathbb F_p$, and define $g$ from these coefficients exactly as in Step 6 there. As shown there, $g\in G_{(m)}=G_{(n-1)}$, formula (1) holds for $g$ over every finite field of characteristic $p$, and $\kappa(h)=\Phi_D(a_0-a_1,a_1-a_2)$.

*$\rho(g)=1$ over $\mathbb F_q$.* Let $v_0,v_1,v_2\in\mathbb F_q^2$ and let $s_0,s_1,s_2$ be indeterminates. Grouping $\kappa(h)$ by multidegree in $(a_0,a_1,a_2)$, as in Step 6 there,
$$\sum_{p_1<p_2}\kappa_{p_1,p_2}(h)(v_0,v_1,v_2)\,s_0^{p_1-1}s_1^{p_2-p_1-1}s_2^{m-p_2}=\Phi_D(s_0v_0-s_1v_1,\,s_1v_1-s_2v_2)\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_q[s_0,s_1,s_2].$$
The factor $\ell$ of (B1) divides $\Phi_D$ in $\mathbb F_q[d_1,d_2]$, and $\ell(s_0v_0-s_1v_1,s_1v_1-s_2v_2)=s_0\,\ell(v_0,0)+s_1\,\ell(-v_1,v_1)+s_2\,\ell(0,-v_2)=0$ by linearity. So the right side is $0$, and every $\kappa_{p_1,p_2}(h)$ vanishes on $(\mathbb F_q^2)^3$. Now let $\rho:G\to U_n(\mathbb F_q)$ be continuous. By Step A, every $S_{p_1,p_2}$ is $0$, so $\langle h,u\rangle=0$ and $\rho(g)=1$ by (1).

*$g\notin G_{(n)}$.* The last paragraph of Step 6 there applies unchanged: as $\Phi_D\ne0$, a decoupled representation over a finite field with more than $D$ elements has $\rho(g)\ne1$, and Step 1 there gives $g\notin G_{(m+1)}=G_{(n)}$. $\square$

**Proposition (the construction cannot do better).** This is proved here. Let $m\ge2$, and let $g\in G_{(m)}$ be any finite product of factors $[w_\mu,w_\nu]^e$ with $|\mu|+|\nu|=m-2$ and, if $p=2$, of squares $w_\mu^2$ with $2|\mu|=m-2$, with $h\in R_m$ as in (1). By (2), $\kappa(h)=\Phi(a_0-a_1,a_1-a_2)$ for a homogeneous $\Phi\in\mathbb F_p[d_1,d_2]$ of degree $m-2$, antisymmetric under $\sigma$ if $p$ is odd and symmetric if $p=2$. Then:

- (a) every continuous $\rho:G\to U_{m+1}(\mathbb F_q)$ kills $g$ if and only if $\Psi_q$ divides $\Phi$;
- (b) $g\notin G_{(m+1)}$ if and only if $\Phi\ne0$.

Consequently, some $g$ of this form lies in $G_{(m)}\setminus G_{(m+1)}$ and is killed by every continuous map to $U_{m+1}(\mathbb F_q)$ if and only if $m+1\ge n_0(q)$. The same holds for elements of $G_{(m)}$ congruent modulo $G_{(m+1)}$ to one of this form, since every continuous $\rho:G\to U_{m+1}(k)$ kills $G_{(m+1)}$ by Step 1 there. So the bound $n_0(q)$ of the theorem, and the bound $n_0$ of [the counterexample](kernel-unipotent-conjecture-fails-for-zp2-free-product-zp.md) for $q=p$, are the best possible for such elements. A smaller bound needs other elements, for instance ones whose leading terms have more than two letters $Z$.

*Proof.* (a) If $\Psi_q$ divides $\Phi$, the argument of Step C applies with $\Phi$ in place of $\Phi_D$, since it used only that every factor of $\Psi_q$ divides $\Phi_D$. Conversely, suppose every such $\rho$ kills $g$. For $p_1<p_2$ and $v_0,v_1,v_2\in\mathbb F_q^2$, the decoupled representation of Step 2 there has $\langle h,u\rangle=\kappa_{p_1,p_2}(h)(v_0,v_1,v_2)$, by Step 4(a) there with all $\lambda_j=1$, so this value is $0$. By the identity displayed in Step C, $\Phi(s_0v_0-s_1v_1,s_1v_1-s_2v_2)=0$ in $\mathbb F_q[s_0,s_1,s_2]$ for all $v_0,v_1,v_2\in\mathbb F_q^2$.

Let $\ell=\lambda_1(d_1)+\lambda_2(d_2)$ be a factor of $\Psi_q$. Choose $v_0,v_1,v_2$ as follows, writing $\bar r$ for an element of $\mathcal R$ other than $r$:

- for $\ell=\delta(r,d_1)$: $v_0=v_1=r$ and $v_2=\bar r$;
- for $\ell=\delta(r,d_2)$: $v_0=\bar r$ and $v_1=v_2=r$;
- for $\ell=\delta(r,d_1+d_2)$: $v_0=v_2=r$ and $v_1=\bar r$;
- for $\ell=\delta(r,d_1)+c\,\delta(r',d_2)$: $v_0=r$, $v_2=r'$, and $v_1\ne0$ in the kernel of the nonzero form $-\delta(r,\cdot)+c\,\delta(r',\cdot)$. Then $v_1$ lies on neither of the lines $\mathbb F_qr$ and $\mathbb F_qr'$: if $\delta(r,v_1)=0$ then $c\,\delta(r',v_1)=0$, so $v_1$ lies on both lines and is $0$, and symmetrically.

In each case $\ell$ vanishes at $e_0=(v_0,0)$, $e_1=(-v_1,v_1)$ and $e_2=(0,-v_2)$, as one checks directly from $\delta(r,r)=\delta(r',r')=0$ and, in the last case, the choice of $v_1$. These vectors of $\mathbb F_q^4$ are linearly independent. Indeed, in a relation $a_0e_0+a_1e_1+a_2e_2=0$ the two components give $a_0v_0=a_1v_1=a_2v_2$. In each case all three $v_i$ are nonzero and two of them are linearly independent, so the coefficients of those two vanish, the common vector is $0$, and the third coefficient vanishes too. So $s\mapsto s_0e_0+s_1e_1+s_2e_2$ is a linear isomorphism from $\bar{\mathbb F}_q^3$ onto $\ker\ell\otimes\bar{\mathbb F}_q$, and $\Phi$ vanishes on $\ker\ell\otimes\bar{\mathbb F}_q$. Complete $\ell$ to a basis $\ell,y_2,y_3,y_4$ of the linear forms over $\mathbb F_q$, and write $\Phi=\ell\,\Phi_1+\Phi_0(y_2,y_3,y_4)$. Then $\Phi_0$ vanishes on $\bar{\mathbb F}_q^3$, so $\Phi_0=0$ and $\ell$ divides $\Phi$.

The $N(q)$ factors of $\Psi_q$ are pairwise non-proportional. Their pairs $(\lambda_1,\lambda_2)$ are $(\delta_r,0)$, $(0,\delta_r)$, $(\delta_r,\delta_r)$ and $(\delta_r,c\,\delta_{r'})$ with $r\ne r'$, writing $\delta_r=\delta(r,\cdot)$. Distinct $r,s\in\mathcal R$ give non-proportional $\delta_r,\delta_s$, and $\delta_r=a\,\delta_r$ forces $a=1$. So if two of these pairs are proportional, they have the same vanishing component, if any; comparing first components, when nonzero, gives the same $r$ and factor $1$; and then comparing second components gives equal pairs, since $\delta_r=c\,\delta_{r'}$ is impossible for $r\ne r'$. Distinct linear forms that are not proportional are non-associate primes of the unique factorization domain $\mathbb F_q[d_1,d_2]$, so their product $\Psi_q$ divides $\Phi$.

(b) If $\Phi\ne0$, the detection argument in the last paragraph of Step 6 there gives $g\notin G_{(m+1)}$. If $\Phi=0$, then $\kappa(h)=0$. For every finite field $k$ of characteristic $p$ and every continuous $\rho:G\to U_{m+1}(k)$, the argument of Step A, which works over every field, shows that each $S_{p_1,p_2}$ is $0$ or a multiple of a value of $\kappa_{p_1,p_2}(h)=0$, so $\rho(g)=1$ by (1). If $g$ were in $G_{(m)}\setminus G_{(m+1)}$, part 1 of [the large-field theorem](kernel-unipotent-property-over-large-finite-fields-for-free-products.md), with any $q'\ge m$, would give a continuous $\rho:G\to U_{m+1}(\mathbb F_{q'})$ with $\rho(g)\ne1$. So $g\in G_{(m+1)}$.

*Consequence.* If $m+1\ge n_0(q)$, the theorem gives such a $g$. Conversely, let $g$ be such an element. By (b), $\Phi\ne0$, and by (a), $\Phi=\Psi_q\Theta$ with $\Theta\ne0$. If $p=2$, then $m-2=\deg\Phi\ge N(q)$. If $p$ is odd, then $\Psi_q(\Theta\circ\sigma)=\Phi\circ\sigma=-\Phi=-\Psi_q\Theta$ by (B3), so $\Theta$ is antisymmetric. A nonzero constant is not antisymmetric when $p$ is odd, so $\deg\Theta\ge1$ and $m-2\ge N(q)+1$. In both cases $m+1\ge n_0(q)$. $\square$

**Remarks.**

- *The mechanism.* Over $\mathbb F_q$, the superdiagonal vectors of commuting matrices lie, run by run, on the $q+1$ lines of $\mathbb F_q^2$, exactly as over $\mathbb F_p$. The only new point is that the group element needs exponents modulo $p$, so the polynomial of Step 5 of the counterexample has to be taken over $\mathbb F_p$. The representatives $(1,t)$ and $(0,1)$ make it Frobenius-stable. Without the descent, the coefficients would lie in $\mathbb F_q$ and would not define an element of $G$.
- *What remains open.* For $2q+3\le n<n_0(q)$ the property over $\mathbb F_q$ is not decided, except that for $q=p$ it holds for $n=2p+3$ and $n=2p+4$, by [the low-degree theorem](zp2-free-product-zp-has-the-kernel-unipotent-property-up-to-size-2p-plus-4.md), and that for $p=2$ it holds for $n\le17$ over every $\mathbb F_q$, by [the dyadic calculation](zp2-free-product-zp-at-p-2-has-the-kernel-unipotent-property-exactly-up-to-size-17.md), which settles $q=2$ completely. By the proposition, settling these cases negatively needs elements that are not congruent modulo $G_{(n)}$ to products of the form used in (1).

**Status.** This answers the question whether one fixed finite coefficient field can detect every Zassenhaus degree of $\mathbb Z_p^2*\mathbb Z_p$: none can. No computer calculation was made for $q>p$; the descent (B2) is proved directly and is also visible in the closed form for $\Psi_q$. No statement of the theorem or the proposition was found in the sources opened for [the conjecture](kernel-unipotent-conjecture.md); no separate literature search was made for the variant over $\mathbb F_q$.
