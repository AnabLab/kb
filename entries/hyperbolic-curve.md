**Definition.** Let $K$ be a field with algebraic closure $\bar K$. A *curve* over $K$ is a smooth, one-dimensional, geometrically connected $K$-scheme. A curve $X$ over $K$ has *type* $(g, r)$ if $X \otimes_K \bar K$ is obtained from a proper curve of genus $g$ over $\bar K$ by removing $r$ distinct $\bar K$-points. It is *hyperbolic* if $2g - 2 + r \ge 1$ [@mochizuki1999, §0, p. 14]. There $K$ is a $\mathbb{Q}_p$-algebra, so it has characteristic $0$. Over every perfect field this definition agrees with the one through a compactification below, but over imperfect fields it is weaker. Over an imperfect field this knowledgebase uses the definition through a compactification.

**Which types are hyperbolic.** This is checked here. If $g \ge 2$, then $2g - 2 \ge 2$. If $g = 1$, the condition is $r \ge 1$, and if $g = 0$, it is $r \ge 3$. So the curves that are not hyperbolic are those of types $(0, 0)$, $(0, 1)$, $(0, 2)$ and $(1, 0)$. Over $\bar K$ these are the projective line, the affine line, the projective line minus two points, and proper curves of genus $1$. Examples of hyperbolic curves are the projective line minus three points, a proper curve of genus at least $2$, and an elliptic curve minus its origin. Mochizuki uses the last of these in the proof that $G_K$ has trivial centre for every subfield $K$ of a finitely generated extension of $\mathbb{Q}_p$ [@mochizuki1999, Lemma 15.8].

**Compactification and cusps.** For a scheme $S$, Mochizuki defines a *family of curves of genus $g$* to be a smooth, proper, geometrically connected morphism $Y \to S$ whose geometric fibres are curves of genus $g$. For $2g - 2 + r > 0$, a *family of hyperbolic curves of type $(g, r)$* is a composite $X \hookrightarrow Y \to S$ of an open immersion onto $Y \setminus D$ and a family $Y \to S$ of curves of genus $g$, where $D \subseteq Y$ is a relative divisor that is finite étale over $S$ of relative degree $r$ [@mochizuki2007cuspidal, §0, p. 4]. If $S$ is normal, then the pair $(Y, D)$ is unique up to canonical isomorphism. When $S$ is the spectrum of a field, Mochizuki notes that this is well known from the elementary theory of algebraic curves [@mochizuki2007cuspidal, §0, pp. 4–5]. He calls $Y$ the *compactification* of $X$ and $D$ its *divisor of cusps* [@mochizuki2007cuspidal, §0, p. 5]. Tame fundamental groups are formed relative to a smooth compactification: Tamagawa forms $\pi_1^{\mathrm{tame}}(U)$ of a curve $U$ with respect to the complement of $U$ in its smooth compactification [@tamagawa1997, §1, pp. 140–141].

*Comparison over a field, checked here.* Let $X$ be a curve over a field $K$. Two standard facts are used: a normal proper curve over $K$ is determined by its function field, and a normal curve is regular.

- Suppose $X = Y \setminus D$ as above, with $S = \mathrm{Spec}\, K$. Then $X$ has type $(g, r)$ in the sense of the definition. Base change to $\bar K$ keeps $Y$ smooth, proper and connected of genus $g$, and it turns the finite étale $K$-scheme $D$ of degree $r$ into $r$ distinct $\bar K$-points.
- Conversely, let $K$ be perfect and let $X$ have type $(g, r)$. Let $Y$ be the normal proper curve over $K$ with the function field of $X$. It contains $X$ as a dense open subscheme. It is regular, and so smooth because $K$ is perfect. It is geometrically connected, because $X \otimes_K \bar K$ is connected and dense in $Y \otimes_K \bar K$. The complement $D = Y \setminus X$ with its reduced structure is a finite reduced $K$-scheme, which is étale because $K$ is perfect. So $Y \otimes_K \bar K$ is the smooth compactification of $X \otimes_K \bar K$. It has genus $g$, and $D$ has degree $r$.

*Over imperfect fields, checked here.* Let $p$ be a prime, $k = \mathbb{F}_p(t)$ and $a = t^{1/p} \in \bar k$. Each curve below has type $(0, 4)$ in the sense of the definition, but it is not a hyperbolic curve in Mochizuki's sense. A compactification $Y$ as in Mochizuki's definition is a normal proper curve with the same function field, so it is the normal compactification, and it is enough to examine that.

- *An inseparable cusp.* Let $P$ be the closed point $x^p = t$ of $\mathbb{P}^1_k$ and $U = \mathbb{P}^1_k \setminus \{0, 1, \infty, P\}$. Then $U \otimes_k \bar k = \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar k} \setminus \{0, 1, \infty, a\}$, and $a \ne 0, 1$. The normal compactification of $U$ is $\mathbb{P}^1_k$. The cusp $P$ has residue field $k(a)$, which is purely inseparable of degree $p$ over $k$, so the divisor of cusps is not étale over $k$.
- *No smooth compactification.* Let $p$ be odd, and let $C$ be the affine curve $y^2 = x^p - t$ over $k$. Over $\bar k$ it becomes $y^2 = (x - a)^p$. The partial derivatives are $-p x^{p-1} = 0$ and $2y$, so the only singular point is $(a, 0)$. It lies over the closed point $P$ of $C$ with ideal $(x^p - t, y)$. Since $x^p - t = y^2$ on $C$, the maximal ideal of $\mathcal{O}_{C, P}$ is generated by $y$. So $C$ is regular, but not smooth at $P$. The map $u \mapsto (a + u^2, u^p)$ from $\mathbb{A}^1_{\bar k}$ to $C \otimes_k \bar k$ is finite and birational, since $u = u^p / (u^2)^{(p-1)/2}$, so it is the normalization. It is bijective, and since $C \otimes_k \bar k$ is smooth away from $(a, 0)$, it is an isomorphism away from $u = 0$. Let $Q$ be the closed point of $C$ over $x = 1$. Its residue field $k(\sqrt{1 - t})$ is separable of degree $2$ over $k$. Put $V = C \setminus \{P, Q\}$. Then $V$ is smooth and geometrically connected over $k$, and in the coordinate $u$, $V \otimes_k \bar k = \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar k} \setminus \{0, \infty, b, -b\}$, where $b^2 = 1 - a$. These four points are distinct, because $b \ne 0$ and $p$ is odd. A smooth proper curve over $k$ that contains $V$ is a normal proper curve with the function field of $C$. It would therefore be the normal compactification of $C$, which contains $C$ and so is not smooth at $P$. Hence $V$ has no smooth compactification at all.

**Convention.** Over an imperfect field, entries in this knowledgebase take a hyperbolic curve of type $(g, r)$ in Mochizuki's sense. It is a curve $X = Y \setminus D$, with $Y$ smooth, proper and geometrically connected of genus $g$, and $D$ finite étale of degree $r$, where $2g - 2 + r > 0$. An entry that means the weaker notion says so.

**Fundamental groups.** Fix a geometric base point of $X$. Write $\Pi^{\mathrm{prf}}_X = \pi_1(X)$ for the étale fundamental group. The structure morphism $X \to \mathrm{Spec}\, K$ induces a map $\Pi^{\mathrm{prf}}_X \to G_K$, whose kernel, the *geometric fundamental group* $\Delta^{\mathrm{prf}}_X$, may be identified with $\pi_1(X \otimes_K \bar K)$ [@mochizuki1999, Introduction, p. 1]. For a prime $p$, let $\Delta_X$ be the maximal pro-$p$ quotient of $\Delta^{\mathrm{prf}}_X$. The kernel of $\Delta^{\mathrm{prf}}_X \to \Delta_X$ is characteristic, hence normal in $\Pi^{\mathrm{prf}}_X$, and dividing by it gives a group $\Pi_X$ with an exact sequence
$$1 \to \Delta_X \to \Pi_X \to G_K \to 1$$
[@mochizuki1999, Introduction, pp. 1–2]. Mochizuki makes the same construction for any $K$-scheme $X$, writing $\Delta^{\mathrm{prf}}_X$ for the kernel of $\pi_1(X) \to G_K$ [@mochizuki1999, Introduction, p. 2]. He writes $\Gamma_K$ for $G_K$, and $\Pi_{X_K}$ for $\Pi_X$.

Conjugation in $\Pi_X$ gives the outer Galois representation $\rho_X : G_K \to \mathrm{Out}(\Delta_X)$. In his §0, where $K$ is a $\mathbb{Q}_p$-algebra, Mochizuki notes, citing Tamagawa, that the extension can be recovered from $\rho_X$ [@mochizuki1999, §0, pp. 14–15]. If $K$ has characteristic $0$, then $\Delta_X$ is topologically finitely generated [@mochizuki1999, §0, p. 15]. More precisely, if $K$ has characteristic $0$ and $X$ has type $(g, r)$, then $\Delta^{\mathrm{prf}}_X$ is the profinite group on $2g + r$ generators with one surface relation, which is free of rank $2g + r - 1$ if $r \ge 1$; see [geometric fundamental groups of curves](geometric-fundamental-groups-of-curves.md).

**Why hyperbolic.** Grothendieck's anabelian conjecture predicts that for certain fields $K$, the functor $X \mapsto (\Pi^{\mathrm{prf}}_X \to G_K)$, with outer homomorphisms over $G_K$ as morphisms, is fully faithful on suitable classes of $K$-varieties, called anabelian. The varieties expected to be anabelian were the sufficiently hyperbolic ones, and hyperbolic curves are the basic case, since their fundamental groups tend to be highly nonabelian [@mochizuki1999, Introduction, pp. 1–2]. For hyperbolic curves over subfields of finitely generated extensions of $\mathbb{Q}_p$, see [Mochizuki's Hom theorem](mochizuki-hom-theorem-for-hyperbolic-curves.md).
